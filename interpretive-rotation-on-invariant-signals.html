Interpretive Rotation on Invariant Signals

A Method for Making Epistemic Architectures Visible

R. Jazinski

Hold the input constant. Rotate the grammar. Observe what changes.

Abstract

This paper introduces the IRIS method (Interpretive Rotation on Invariant Signals) — a procedurally explicit analytical protocol for making visible the interpretive architectures through which shared evidence is processed into divergent conclusions. The method treats interpretive frameworks as grammars: rule-bound processing systems with five explicit parameters that process the same evidence into divergent conclusions. The analyst holds the empirical input constant, rotates the grammar, and observes what changes in the output. The paper specifies the method’s formal architecture, demonstrates it through a worked example with full parameter specification for the three grammars compared, summarises a preliminary convergence programme involving 15 distinctively prompted large-language-model instances across 20 case studies, and positions the method relative to discourse analysis, frame analysis, Q-methodology, analytical pluralism, argumentation theory, and the sociology of knowledge. The IRIS method’s contribution is procedural: it supplies an explicit grammar notation and a controlled rotation protocol that lets analysts declare, apply, and contest interpretive architectures on identical empirical inputs rather than merely describe them.

The method · Section 1

Introduction

Why do people examining the same evidence reach incompatible conclusions? One common family of explanations attributes divergence to bias, motivated cognition, or informational asymmetries. This paper develops a complementary claim: even when relevant empirical reference points are shared, divergence can be generated by differences in the processing rules that select, weight, and close over those reference points. The claim is not that bias is irrelevant but that bias often operates through interpretive architectures whose rules can be made explicit and compared. This is a methodological paper: it specifies a procedure for making those architectures visible.

The claim is intended to be broadly applicable. In many domains where shared evidence generates conflicting interpretations — climate policy, public health, economic reform, geopolitical strategy — the divergence can often be traced, at least in part, to the rules governing what counts as a signal, how signals are weighted, and what forms of conclusion are admissible. In many real-world disputes, both evidence and architectures differ simultaneously; IRIS models the limiting case in which the empirical substrate is stipulated as invariant by the analyst, isolating architectural effects that in practice are entangled with informational asymmetries. These rules constitute what this paper calls an interpretive grammar: a structured processing architecture that transforms empirical input into analytical output. Different grammars, applied to the same input, produce different outputs. (The term “grammar” is used here to denote a rule-bound processing system, not in the linguistic sense of syntactic or semantic structure.)

The IRIS method — Interpretive Rotation on Invariant Signals — formalises this observation as a procedurally explicit analytical protocol: hold the input constant, rotate the grammar, and observe what changes in the output. Fuller operational protocols are available in the IRIS Toolkit, and an exploratory convergence archive is documented in the IRIS Case Studies series. his paper presents the method’s core architecture in a self-contained format: a reader should be able to understand and provisionally evaluate — and, in straightforward cases, sketch an application of — the method from this paper alone, consulting the companion publications for fuller operational detail and case study evidence.

The method does not determine which interpretation is correct. Its purpose is to make the processing architectures visible so that evaluation can proceed with clearer awareness of what each grammar selects, excludes, and permits. This need not entail relativism. One cannot judge what one cannot see. The IRIS method aims to make the machinery of interpretation visible, and in doing so can help render opaque disagreement into structured, analysable divergence.

The civic dimension deserves emphasis. Contemporary debates about polarisation, misinformation, and epistemic crisis often emphasise information access (filter bubbles, echo chambers) or individual cognition (confirmation bias, motivated reasoning). The IRIS method proposes a different diagnostic: divergence often has a structural component, not merely an informational one. Citizens on opposing sides of a contested issue may be processing the same evidence through different interpretive grammars whose selection rules, weighting priorities, and closure mechanisms can generate incompatible outputs even when empirical reference points are shared. If this diagnostic is correct, then fact-checking and media literacy — however valuable in their own right — may be insufficient alone to resolve the divergence, because the divergence is not produced by factual error alone. It is produced, at least in part, by the architecture of interpretation itself. Whether IRIS can contribute to real-world depolarisation remains an open question pending participatory testing; what it offers now is a diagnostic instrument for making the architecture of disagreement visible — a step towards more informed engagement in which disputants can identify not merely what they disagree about but which processing rules generate the disagreement.

The paper proceeds as follows. Section 2 defines the grammar concept and its five parameters with operational specifications. Section 3 situates grammars relative to neighbouring constructs, including paradigms, epistemes, thought-styles, ideal types, and orders of worth. Section 4 demonstrates the method through a worked example with full parameter specification. Section 5 summarises a preliminary convergence programme and addresses its limitations. Section 6 addresses the ontological status of signals. Section 7 discusses the observer’s grammar. Section 8 positions the method relative to adjacent approaches. Section 9 provides a compact protocol and discusses scope conditions. Section 10 concludes.

The method · Section 2

The Grammar Concept

An interpretive grammar, as deployed in this method, is a rule-bound processing system defined by five parameters. Following Weber’s (1949) conception of ideal types, grammars are analytical instruments constructed by the analyst to maximise interpretive contrast, not ethnographic descriptions of how actual communities experience their own reasoning. Whether analyst-specified grammars correspond to the processing rules of naturally occurring interpretive communities is an empirical question that the method does not claim to have answered; establishing that correspondence is a future research direction outlined in Section 9.

The five core parameters are:

Primary analytical variable (V)

The factor the grammar treats as causally fundamental — the lens through which all other signals are read. For example: class structure (egalitarian grammar), cultural continuity (civilisational grammar), or systemic transformation (structural grammar). V determines what the grammar is ultimately “about.”

Signal selection rules (S)

Inclusion and exclusion criteria governing which empirical inputs the grammar admits for processing. S operates as a filter: inputs that pass the criteria enter the grammar; inputs that fail are excluded or demoted. The analyst must declare what is admitted and what is excluded, with justification. For example, an egalitarian grammar admits signals mappable to capital concentration and institutional capture; it excludes or reinterprets signals grounded in cultural identity as independent causal forces.

Weighting priority (W)

An ordered priority ranking that determines which admitted signals are treated as primary and which as secondary or background. W is not a numeric weighting function but an explicit ranking rule: signal A takes precedence over signal B when both are present. For the egalitarian grammar, economic magnitude (wealth share, lobbying expenditure) outranks cultural indicators; for the civilisational grammar, moral valence (relationship to foundational values) outranks distributional metrics.

Causal chain type (C)

The form of explanation the grammar can construct. C constrains the admissible logic connecting signals to conclusions. The analyst declares the causal chain type appropriate to their domain. Based on regularities observed in the preliminary case-study archive (Section 5), three broad families recur: agentic/moral chains (agents or moral forces drive outcomes through intentional or normative mechanisms), structural/systemic chains (institutional or material processes produce outcomes without requiring moral attribution), and non-agentic correlational chains (co-variation among indicators is mapped without attributing causality or moral agency). Analysts may specify alternative types — teleological, functional, narrative, conjunctural — as appropriate; the typology is open and illustrative, not exhaustive. The requirement is explicit declaration, not conformity to a fixed taxonomy.

Admissible closure (A)

The set of permissible conclusion-forms the grammar can reach. A defines the boundary of what the grammar is allowed to conclude. Closure is not a single conclusion but a class of admissible outputs: for an egalitarian grammar, the admissible set includes reform, rupture, or collective renewal; it excludes conclusions in which the current order is sustainable without redistribution. For a structural grammar, the admissible set includes trend description and phase-transition identification; it excludes moral prescription. A functions as a stopping rule: the grammar reaches closure when its processing has produced an output within the admissible set.

The grammar can be formally represented as G = (V, S, W, C, A). For fuller specification, four optional parameters extend the core schema: excluded signals (E), characteristic vocabulary (K; the distinctive lexicon a grammar employs), temporal orientation (T; e.g. past/present/future salience), and temporal formation (F; e.g. linear/cyclical/crisis framing). Of these, E is used in the worked example below; K, T, and F were not required for the present demonstration. The formal definition and full operational protocols are developed in the IRIS Toolkit (iris.rodrigojazinski.com); the core schema presented here is sufficient for provisional application.

What makes grammars architectural rather than merely perspectival? Five properties distinguish the grammar concept from the looser notion of a “perspective” or “worldview.” First, grammars are parametrically specified ex ante: the analyst declares V, S, W, C, and A before processing begins, not after. Second, grammars constrain outputs: given a declared signal set, the grammar’s rules constrain what closures are reachable and what closures are excluded. Third, grammars are comparable across domains using a common parameter schema: the same five-parameter structure is intended to be portable across domains — whether the domain is American democracy, antimicrobial resistance, or cryptocurrency regulation — though demonstration remains domain-specific at this stage. Fourth, grammars can be rotated on the same signal set, producing different outputs from identical inputs under controlled conditions. Fifth, grammars specified as (V, S, W, C, A) can in principle be operated by another analyst on the same signal set, enabling independent re-application and contestation. These properties — pre-specification, output constraint, cross-domain comparability, rotational operation, and re-applicability by independent analysts — are what the architectural metaphor denotes. A perspective does not require them; a grammar does.

The method · Section 3

Grammars and Neighbouring Constructs

The grammar concept inherits from, but is not identical to, several established constructs. Situating IRIS within this intellectual lineage clarifies both what it borrows and what it adds.

Kuhn’s paradigms (1962) demonstrated that frameworks shape what counts as evidence. Grammars inherit this insight but differ operationally: paradigms are tacitly shared by communities and identifiable only retrospectively; grammars are explicitly specified by the analyst and operable prospectively. Paradigms govern practice within a single community; grammars coexist by design, enabling controlled rotation.

Hacking’s styles of reasoning (1982) showed that entire domains of inquiry create the categories of truth they then discover. The grammar concept operationalises this: where Hacking describes styles as historical formations to be excavated, IRIS specifies grammars as processing architectures to be declared, tested, and contested.

Foucault’s epistemes (1966) established that knowledge systems have structural rules determining what can be said and thought within them. The grammar concept shares this recognition but treats the rules as analyst-declared processing instruments whose consequences can be demonstrated under controlled input conditions, rather than as unconscious conditions of possibility to be excavated archaeologically.

Mannheim’s thought-styles (1929/1936) and Fleck’s thought collectives (1935/1979) are the grammar concept’s most direct precursors in the sociology of knowledge. Mannheim demonstrated that the social position of the knower shapes the knowledge produced; Fleck showed that thought collectives develop processing rules that determine what counts as a fact within their community. The IRIS method extends their insight from social position to explicit processing architecture: it specifies the rules as declarable parameters rather than as socially embedded dispositions, enabling controlled comparison across grammars that Mannheim’s and Fleck’s frameworks describe but do not operationalise.

From Haraway’s situated knowledges (1988) and Harding’s standpoint epistemology (1986), the method inherits the claim that objectivity is not achieved by transcending position but by making position explicit and accountable. The IRIS method’s reflexive declaration (Step O) operationalises this insight.

Boltanski and Thévenot’s orders of worth (2006) map justification regimes — civic, market, industrial, and others — that function as rule-bound systems processing the same events into different normative conclusions. The grammar concept shares their recognition of structured interpretive plurality but differs in its procedural ambition: where Boltanski and Thévenot describe which regimes actors mobilise in practice, IRIS provides a controlled rotation protocol enabling systematic comparison of how each architecture processes identical inputs.

The grammar concept’s originality therefore lies not in the core insight — that interpretive frameworks shape conclusions — which has been articulated in various forms from Mannheim through Kuhn to Haraway. The originality lies in the operational constellation: a parametric specification schema (V, S, W, C, A), a controlled rotation protocol on declared invariant signals, and a reflexive declaration requirement (Step O) that makes the analyst’s own grammar visible and auditable. It is this constellation, not any single element, that constitutes the IRIS method’s distinctive contribution.

Demonstration · Section 4

Worked Example: Citizens United v. FEC (2010)

Step O: Author’s reflexive declaration. The author’s home grammar is structural-systemic: V = systemic transformation as measurable process; S = admits quantifiable, longitudinally trackable signals, demotes signals requiring moral attribution; W = measurability and systemic coupling outrank moral valence; C = correlational (co-variation mapped without assigning moral agency); A = description and phase-transition identification only, excludes moral prescription. The structural grammar is the author’s default analytical lens, producing the highest fluency and the greatest resistance to abandonment under rotation. One traceable consequence: the structural grammar’s exclusion of moral vocabulary in the worked example below is the output the author found most natural to produce; the moral-causal chains of the egalitarian and civilisational grammars required deliberate ventriloquism, which I experienced as lower fluency and greater effort in composing the closure sentences.

The following demonstration applies the IRIS method to the U.S. Supreme Court’s Citizens United v. Federal Election Commission decision of 2010, which held unconstitutional prohibitions on corporate and union independent expenditures and electioneering communications and did not affect the ban on direct corporate contributions. The purpose is not to settle a political question but to show how the same event becomes three different analytical objects depending on the processing architecture applied.

Step 1: The invariant signal set

Four signals are held constant across all three grammars: (i) the 5–4 decision and its legal reasoning holding that prohibitions on corporate independent expenditures and electioneering communications violate the First Amendment; (ii) post-decision spending data as reported in FEC disclosures (independent expenditures and electioneering communications rose from the low hundreds of millions in the 2007–2008 cycle to about $1.27 billion in the 2011–2012 cycle, per FEC reporting categories); (iii) the Gilens and Page (2014) conclusion that, in their multivariate analysis, average citizens have little or no independent influence on policy outcomes once economic elites and organised interest groups are accounted for; (iv) a widely discussed “revolving-door” dynamic (personnel movement between regulatory agencies and relevant industries). For the purposes of this demonstration, these signals are stipulated as factual (treated as invariant) across the three grammars under comparison; each grammar assigns them different significance.

Step 3: Rotation and outputs

Egalitarian grammar. The grammar selects the economic concentration dimension as primary. Signal (ii) (independent-expenditure and electioneering-communications spending) and signal (iii) (Gilens and Page) are weighted highest. Under this grammar, the decision is processed as the juridical crystallisation of oligarchic capture — a moment at which capital’s influence over the democratic process is construed as having received formal constitutional protection. The libertarian free-speech argument (signal excluded by S) is suppressed. Closure (under this grammar): the decision is read as a formal displacement of democratic sovereignty by corporate sovereignty. This closure falls within A (reform/rupture required).

Civilisational grammar. The grammar selects the sovereignty-violation dimension. Signal (i) (the legal reasoning) is weighted highest for its moral valence. Under this grammar, the decision is processed as elite betrayal: a perceived corruption of a constitutional principle by actors framed as indifferent to the national community the Constitution was designed to protect. The economic data (signals ii, iii) are admitted but subordinated to moral framing. Closure (under this grammar): the decision is read as institutions turned against the people they were designed to serve. This closure falls within A (restoration required).

Structural grammar.Under this grammar, both moral attributions are stripped. All four signals are admitted and weighted by measurability. The decision is logged as a data point in a trend line alongside lobbying expenditure, policy responsiveness, and revolving-door data. No causal agents are named. Closure (under this grammar): the political system is described as increasingly serving elite preferences, with democratic participation functioning at a symbolic level. This closure falls within A (description only).

What the rotation reveals

The three grammars share the same four signals but produce genuinely different analytical objects. The egalitarian grammar constitutes an object called “oligarchic capture”; the civilisational grammar constitutes an object called “elite betrayal of the covenant”; the structural grammar constitutes an object called “a phase-transition indicator.” Analytically, these function less as three descriptions of one object than as three distinct objects constituted from shared material — a pattern that appeared to recur in the preliminary convergence programme across multiple domains. In practice, actors often move between grammars, adopting egalitarian framing in one context and civilisational framing in another; IRIS could in principle model such movement but does not do so here.

The three grammars correspond to recognisable interpretive traditions, which is expected for a mature political controversy. The method’s discovery capacity is better demonstrated in emerging domains: in the cryptocurrency case (Section 5), the preliminary programme tentatively identified and coded a “Protocol Commons” grammar still in formation, with our coding suggesting fragmentation into five variants between testing rounds — a pattern the IRIS apparatus can highlight by requiring parametric specification across rounds, revealing fragmentation that might otherwise appear as noise. The IRIS method’s contribution is not the discovery of established traditions but their parametric formalisation in a common schema that enables controlled comparison. A competent analyst could generate three plausible readings of Citizens United without IRIS; what IRIS adds is a way to specify the processing rules that produce each reading, identify which rules generate the divergence, and document what each grammar must exclude to maintain its coherence.

To see the rotation’s diagnostic power more precisely, consider how a single signal — the Gilens and Page (2014) finding that average citizens have little or no independent influence on policy outcomes once economic elites and organised interest groups are accounted for — is processed differently by each grammar. The egalitarian grammar treats this as its highest-weighted signal, reading it as confirmation that the democratic feedback loop has been captured by capital: the finding is the empirical core of the oligarchic capture diagnosis. The civilisational grammar admits the same finding but subordinates it to its moral-causal frame: the policy-responsiveness deficit is a symptom of elite betrayal, not its cause; the cause is the abandonment of civic duty and the corruption of constitutional principles. The structural grammar strips both attributions and reads the finding as one data point in a correlational trend: policy responsiveness is a measurable variable that has shifted from one configuration to another, without moral vocabulary. The same empirical finding enters three architectures and produces three different analytical roles — core evidence, subordinate symptom, and neutral data point — depending entirely on which processing rules are in operation.

The excluded signals are equally revealing. The egalitarian grammar excludes the libertarian free-speech argument — the claim that removing spending limits expands First Amendment protections for all participants, including small-business owners and advocacy groups. This signal is available in the empirical record but inadmissible under the grammar’s selection rules, which require all signals to be readable as effects or instruments of economic power. The civilisational grammar excludes the same signal on different grounds: not because it is economically irrelevant but because it is morally insufficient — a procedural defence of a substantive betrayal. The structural grammar excludes the free-speech argument because it requires qualitative moral interpretation rather than quantitative measurement. Three grammars, three different reasons for suppressing the same signal. The IRIS method makes these exclusions explicit in a standardised way; without explicit parameterisation, they can remain embedded in the architecture and easy to miss.

Finally, the example illustrates grammar aging. The structural grammar can reach closure immediately: the spending data and policy-responsiveness study are sufficient to describe the system’s configuration. The egalitarian grammar forms its closure more slowly, requiring the accumulation of downstream effects (subsequent election cycles, widening donor-class influence) to confirm the oligarchic capture diagnosis. The civilisational grammar’s closure is future-oriented: it predicts further institutional decay unless restoration occurs, and its closure remains provisionally open as long as the nation retains the capacity for cultural renewal. The three grammars close at different moments in the evidence cycle — a pattern that emerged in the convergence programme across multiple domains as grammar aging.

Demonstration · Section 5

Preliminary Convergence Programme

The IRIS method’s operational feasibility has been explored through a preliminary programme in which 15 distinctively prompted large-language-model instances processed 20 case studies across 17 domains (full methods, prompts, and raw outputs in IRIS Case Studies: Selected Analysis). The programme is reported here to illustrate feasibility and preliminary findings; it does not constitute full methodological validation, which requires human-participant replication.

Agents were prompted to identify the dominant interpretive frameworks operating in a given domain and to specify their processing rules. A typical prompt took the form: “Given the following empirical signals [listed], identify two to four structurally distinct ways in which these signals could be interpreted, and for each, specify the primary organising principle, the signals emphasised or excluded, the form of causal reasoning employed, and the range of conclusions the interpretation permits.” Prompts did not use IRIS terminology and did not instruct agents to search for “grammars.” Agent outputs were then hand-coded into V, S, W, C, A structures using a common coding scheme; structural equivalence was explored by comparing parameter values across agents for each domain. Under these conditions, agents repeatedly produced architectures that could plausibly be mapped onto recognisable grammar families across the 20 cases examined in this preliminary programme. Given that prompts were designed to elicit structural distinctions, this convergence partly reflects prompt design; the finding is that agents repeatedly produced architectures mappable to the IRIS schema, suggesting discursive stability rather than independent validation. One agent (SciSpace), in a test with zero exposure to the method’s vocabulary, independently named architectures as “Egalitarian-Distributive,” “Civilizational-Institutional,” and “Structural-Systemic.”

A circularity concern must be addressed directly. Large language models are trained on corpora saturated with the interpretive traditions the method claims to identify. Their convergence on recognisable grammar architectures may reflect the statistical structure of their training data rather than an independent validation of the method’s claims (Bender et al., 2021). This concern is why the convergence programme is treated as computational plausibility evidence, not validation. The programme’s design mitigates this partially — agents were not primed with IRIS terminology, and convergence held across domains as diverse as antimicrobial resistance and cryptocurrency regulation — but it cannot eliminate the concern. One plausible interpretation is that the convergence offers a computational indication that the grammar architectures may correspond to relatively stable structures, pending human-participant replication. It is not evidence that grammars have ontological status independent of the discourse that produces them. Human-participant validation — in which self-identified interpretive communities co-construct grammar definitions and test their processing predictions against new signals — is the necessary next stage.

Five preliminary patterns emerged. First, grammar convergence: across the 20 cases tested, agents configured without IRIS terminology produced architectures codable into comparable grammar families. Second, ontological pluralism: grammars did not merely process the same object differently; they constituted different objects from shared empirical material. Third, emerging grammars: in some domains (e.g., cryptocurrency), the programme surfaced grammars still in formation, with the “Protocol Commons” grammar fragmenting into five distinct variants between testing rounds. Fourth, grammar aging: grammars formed their closures at different moments in the evidence cycle — some closing before the evidence was fully in (pre-evidential closure), others adjusting as data accumulated (evidence-responsive closure), and still others requiring decades of data before settling (post-evidential closure). Fifth, observer effects: across the agents tested, each exhibited a stable “home grammar” that influenced its processing across domains, identified by analysing which grammar each agent defaulted to without prompting, produced with greatest fluency, and showed most resistance to abandoning under rotation. In this exploratory finding from the AI consciousness case, each of the agents tested placed Computational Functionalism first, and reported Biological Naturalism as hardest to operate — a pattern consistent with the possibility that the observer’s grammar is a structural feature of interpretation, not a personal idiosyncrasy, though replication with larger and more diverse agent samples is needed.

The antimicrobial resistance (AMR) case illustrates ontological pluralism with particular clarity. The signal set comprised WHO surveillance data on resistant infections, pharmaceutical R&D pipeline figures, antibiotic consumption statistics, and mortality projections. Under non-primed prompt conditions (i.e., without IRIS terminology), agents converged on three grammars: Biomedical-Clinical (AMR as a treatment failure requiring new drugs and stewardship protocols), Economic-Market (AMR as a market failure where antibiotics are underpriced and R&D is underfunded), and Evolutionary-Ecological (AMR as an ecological inevitability driven by selection pressure in microbial populations). The same clinical situation — a patient dying of an MRSA bloodstream infection — can be constituted, under different grammars, as a treatment failure, a market failure, or an ecological inevitability — from the IRIS perspective, three different objects constituted from the same clinical signals. The finding suggests that ontological pluralism is not confined to political interpretation: it operates wherever multiple processing architectures are structurally available, including in domains grounded in natural science.

Boundaries · Section 6

The Ontological Status of Signals

A constructivist objection arises: if grammars shape what counts as a signal, then signals cannot be held invariant across grammars. The IRIS method does not require a philosophical resolution of the realism debate. It requires only a methodological stipulation: that for the purposes of a given analysis, certain inputs can be treated as stable because the grammars under comparison do not dispute them. This is an operational commitment, not a metaphysical claim.

The stipulation has affinities with critical realism’s (Bhaskar, 1975) commitment to a stratified reality accessible through but not reducible to social practice, and with entity realism’s (Hacking, 1983) emphasis on manipulable entities rather than theoretical unification. Unlike critical realism, IRIS makes no claim about stratified ontology; unlike entity realism, it does not require manipulability. The method differs from both in focus: it is not primarily concerned with the ontological status of the entities under investigation but with the processing architectures through which those entities become interpretively significant. The finding that different grammars constitute different analytical objects from shared material resonates with Mol’s (2002) demonstration of ontological multiplicity in medical practice, though IRIS differs in providing a controlled comparison protocol rather than ethnographic description.

A concrete example clarifies the scope. In the Citizens United case, all three grammars accept that post-decision independent expenditures and electioneering communications rose to nearly $1.3 billion in 2011–2012. They disagree on what this fact means, not on whether it occurred. The spending figure is methodologically stabilised: it functions as an invariant input for the duration of the rotation. Where grammars disagree on the existence of a signal itself — where one grammar treats a putative signal as artefact or fabrication — the method’s invariance claim weakens. In such cases, the contestation of the signal set becomes part of the IRIS finding (a distinct class of divergence) rather than an assumption the method requires. The method is strongest when applied to domains where the empirical substrate is accepted across interpretive communities even as its significance is contested. Signal invariance exists on a spectrum: the method operates most reliably when signals are highly institutionalised (a court ruling, GDP data, a clinical measurement) and may struggle when signals are epistemically fragile or heavily contested at the point of data collection.

A note on an internal tension the reader may have noticed: the paper claims “no signal is neutral” (because all signals are selected and weighted by grammars) while also requiring signals to be “held invariant.” These claims are compatible. “Invariant” means methodologically stabilised — accepted across grammars for the duration of the analysis — not ontologically neutral. The signals entering the rotation carry no built-in interpretation, but they are not theory-free; they are theory-stable within the scope of the comparison.

Three boundary cases clarify the method’s limits. First, phenomena where grammars constitute rather than interpret: in debates over gender identity, for instance, the category structure itself is contested — one grammar treats gender as a biological binary, another as a social construction, a third as a spectrum of lived experience. These are not three interpretations of the same signal; they are three different signal ontologies. The IRIS method can document this divergence but cannot treat any one ontology as the invariant input. Second, phenomena where signal identification is itself the dispute: in debates over what constitutes “terrorism” versus “resistance,” the classification of the act is the contested variable, not its interpretation once classified. Third, phenomena where the empirical record is too thin or too contested to stabilise: emerging scientific controversies in which the data itself is preliminary and subject to rapid revision. In each case, the analyst’s responsibility is to assess whether signal constancy is defensible before proceeding. If it is not, the appropriate response is to document why the domain falls outside the method’s current scope — a finding that is itself analytically valuable because it identifies the boundary between interpretive divergence (where grammars process the same material differently) and ontological divergence (where grammars constitute different material).

Boundaries · Section 7

The Observer’s Grammar

The analyst who constructs the grammars, selects the signals, and operates the rotation is not standing outside the interpretive field. The analyst has a grammar too. In the convergence programme, each LLM instance exhibited a stable pattern in the grammars it produced most fluently — what the method calls a home grammar — that influenced its processing across domains.

The IRIS method’s response is not to deny this condition but to make it inspectable. The reflexive declaration (Step O) requires the analyst, before beginning the rotation, to specify their own home grammar using the same parametric form (V, S, W, C, A) applied to the grammars under study. This declaration is published alongside the analysis, providing readers with an indicative, qualitative index of interpretive distance: the degree of divergence between the analyst’s home grammar and each operated grammar. The convergence programme found that this distance is often apparent — outputs for home grammars tended to be more fluent, more analytically confident, and more internally coherent than outputs for distant grammars.

When an analyst operates a grammar they do not inhabit, their processing carries traces of their home grammar — a phenomenon the method calls ventriloquism (letting the grammar, not the analyst, speak the conclusion inherent to its architecture, with inevitable residual traces of the analyst’s own processing). This ventriloquism is not a methodological embarrassment to be concealed but an expected consequence of situated interpretation. The method treats it as an object of analysis: a pattern to be documented and scrutinised rather than denied. The analyst’s inevitable interpretive blind spots become auditable constraints when the home grammar is declared, rather than hidden confounders when it is not. Declaration renders bias inspectable — an epistemic advance, though not a panacea.

A deeper limitation is that the analyst’s home grammar may shape not only the processing of rival grammars but the selection of which grammars are included in the rotation — a form of bias that declaration alone cannot correct. The recommended mitigation is multi-analyst rotation, in which researchers with different declared home grammars independently specify and operate grammars on the same signal set and compare outputs; this is proposed as a standard for high-stakes applications in the participatory validation programme (Section 9).

Boundaries · Section 8

Positioning Relative to Adjacent Approaches

The IRIS method intersects with several established traditions. The following positioning emphasises what each tradition contributes and where IRIS diverges, avoiding straw-man contrasts.

Discourse analysis (Fairclough, 1995; van Dijk, 1993) traces how language produces meaning within and across frameworks, typically elaborating interpretive logics inductively from texts rather than through pre-specified processing architectures. The IRIS method compares multiple architectures by requiring each to be declared ex ante as a parametric specification. The distinction is procedural: IRIS pre-specifies and rotates; discourse analysis typically elaborates and compares.

Frame analysis (Entman, 1993; Benford and Snow, 2000) identifies selection and salience processes that promote particular problem definitions, causal interpretations, and treatment recommendations. IRIS shares this attention to selection and salience but adds three requirements absent from standard framing methodology: parametric pre-specification of the full grammar tuple (V, S, W, C, A), controlled rotation on a declared invariant signal set, and explicit admissible closure constraints. Frame analysis can operate without these; IRIS cannot.

Argumentation theory (Toulmin, 1958) decomposes inferential structure into data, warrants, backings, and rebuttals. Toulmin’s “warrants” map closely onto IRIS’s causal chain types (C), and his “field-dependent” standards of assessment anticipate the grammar concept’s domain-sensitivity. IRIS differs in scope: where Toulmin analyses the structure of individual arguments, IRIS analyses the processing architecture of entire interpretive systems and compares them under controlled conditions.

Q-methodology (Stephenson, 1953; Watts and Stenner, 2012) discovers interpretive patterns empirically through factor analysis of participant responses. IRIS specifies processing architectures in advance and tests their consequences. The two approaches are complementary: Q-methodology could empirically validate whether analyst-specified grammars correspond to naturally occurring interpretive communities, providing the participatory validation that the convergence programme cannot.

Analytical pluralism in philosophy of science (Cartwright, 1999; Mitchell, 2003; Longino, 2002) proposes that complex phenomena may require multiple explanatory frameworks. IRIS extends pluralist insights to qualitative interpretive-political analysis, providing an operational procedure that makes pluralism not merely a philosophical commitment but a practicable analytical method.

Orders of worth (Boltanski and Thévenot, 2006) map justification regimes — civic, market, industrial, and others — that actors mobilise in disputes. These regimes function as rule-bound grammars processing the same events into different normative conclusions. IRIS’s distinction lies in its experimental dimension: where Boltanski and Thévenot’s framework is primarily sociological (mapping which regimes are mobilised in practice), IRIS provides a controlled rotation protocol that enables systematic comparison of how each architecture processes identical inputs.

Multiverse and specification curve analysis (Steegen et al., 2016) in quantitative methods holds data constant while varying analytic choices to reveal how conclusions depend on researcher degrees of freedom. IRIS is a qualitative-interpretive analogue of this approach, holding empirical inputs constant while varying interpretive processing rules rather than statistical model specifications.

Interpretive political science (Bevir and Rhodes, 2003) treats political action as meaningful practice requiring hermeneutic understanding. IRIS adds an architectural dimension: it requires that interpretive frameworks be declared as parametric processing systems whose consequences can be demonstrated under controlled conditions.

Epistemic injustice (Fricker, 2007) identifies hermeneutical injustice as the inability to make sense of one’s experience due to gaps in shared interpretive resources. If grammars exclude certain signals, this resonates with Fricker’s diagnostic. The IRIS method does not resolve epistemic injustice, but by making excluded-signal sets explicit and auditable, it provides a tool for diagnosing which voices and which experiences a given grammar structurally suppresses.

Social epistemology (Goldman, 1999; Bloor, 1976) asks which social practices reliably produce true beliefs (Goldman) and whether truth and falsity should be explained symmetrically by social causes (Bloor). IRIS is explicitly non-adjudicative: it does not determine which grammar produces truer beliefs. Its contribution is diagnostic — making processing rules visible so that normative evaluation can be conducted with a more explicit account of each grammar’s architecture. Goldman’s veritistic question remains open after an IRIS analysis; the analysis provides the architectural map on which that question can be more precisely posed.

Protocol · Section 9

Scope Conditions, Protocol, and Future Directions

When to use IRIS

The method is appropriate when: the domain is one in which multiple interpretive architectures demonstrably operate; the empirical substrate is stable enough across grammars that the “same signals” claim is defensible; and the analyst can specify at least two grammars with enough operational precision that their processing rules can be declared and contested. It is inappropriate when grammars constitute rather than interpret the phenomena (signal constancy breaks down), or when the research question concerns meaning-recovery rather than architectural comparison. The quality of an IRIS analysis depends on the quality of the grammar specifications: poorly constructed or under-specified grammars will produce uninformative rotations.

Compact protocol

Step O — Reflexive declaration. Before beginning, the analyst specifies their home grammar in parametric form (V, S, W, C, A) and publishes this alongside the analysis.

Step 1 — Define the observation field. Identify the phenomenon or signal set to be analysed.

Step 2 — Construct the invariant signal set. Specify which empirical inputs are held constant, with sources and justification for their cross-grammar stability.

Step 3 — Specify the grammars. For each grammar, declare V, S, W, C, A, and E.

Step 4 — Rotate and process. Apply each grammar to the same signal set. Document which signals are emphasised, which are demoted, and how the causal chain produces closure.

Step 5 — Compare closures. Document divergence across grammars. Identify which parameter differences generate the divergence.

Step 6 — Identify counter-signals and leakage. Examine signals that resist processing within each grammar. Document moments where grammars borrow from neighbouring architectures under interpretive pressure.

Future directions

The IRIS method is designed to develop along three trajectories. The first is cumulative case study production: new domains are intended to be analysed quarterly and published in the IRIS Case Studies series, expanding the empirical base and testing the method’s predictions across an ever-wider range of interpretive landscapes. The second is longitudinal grammar tracking: existing cases could be re-run annually to test whether grammars remain stable, consolidate, fragment, or migrate — converting the cross-sectional findings on grammar aging and emerging grammars into time-series data. The Brexit case has already been processed at two distinct moments (Rounds 2 and 4), revealing observable grammar landscape evolution; an annual re-run programme would systematise this observation across the full archive.

The third and most consequential trajectory is participatory grammar specification with human communities — the essential next stage that would move the method from procedural convergence (demonstrated by the AI programme) to interpretive validity (not yet established). Participatory specification would involve working with self-identified interpretive communities to co-construct grammar definitions from the inside, then testing whether those definitions predict the community’s actual processing of new signals. This procedure would answer the question that the current method leaves open: whether analyst-specified grammars correspond to the processing rules of naturally occurring interpretive communities, or whether they are analytical constructs that capture structural patterns without mapping onto lived experience. Both outcomes would be informative: correspondence would strengthen the method’s empirical grounding; divergence would reveal where the analyst’s specification over-simplifies or distorts the community’s actual architecture, which is itself a valuable finding.

Longer-term extensions include computational implementation in which grammar specifications are encoded as processing rules and applied algorithmically to signal sets, and integration with the author’s doctoral research into how constraint systems — from divination and strategy games to algorithmic risk scoring and credit assessment — convert uncertainty into authoritative closure along an interpretive–syntactic spectrum. The IRIS method is the interpretive-political instance of this broader programme. The full method architecture, operational protocols, and convergence evidence are developed in a forthcoming companion volume, which demonstrates the method on a sustained case study with three fully operated grammars, six micro-case studies, and the complete convergence findings. The “IRIS Toolkit” provides signal coding procedures, grammar derivation guidelines, convergence assessment criteria, and a structured reflexive declaration template. The “IRIS Case Studies: Selected Analysis” presents the convergence evidence in full. Companion materials are available at iris.rodrigojazinski.com; the core method as presented in this paper is sufficient for provisional application and independent evaluation.

Protocol · Section 10

Conclusion

This paper has presented the IRIS method: a procedure for holding empirical signals constant, rotating interpretive grammars, and observing how different processing architectures produce divergent conclusions from identical inputs. The method’s distinctive contribution is procedural: it supplies an explicit five-parameter grammar notation and a controlled rotation protocol that lets analysts declare, apply, and contest interpretive architectures on identical empirical inputs rather than merely describe them. Grammars are analytical instruments provisionally constructed by the analyst, not psychological claims about how communities process information; whether they correspond to naturally occurring interpretive architectures is an empirical question for participatory validation.

If we treat ideology as a processing architecture, if we accept that signals are never neutral, and if we acknowledge the observer’s methodologically inescapable position within any IRIS rotation, then conventional responses to epistemic polarisation may address the wrong level of the problem. When divergence is driven primarily by processing-rule differences, information interventions alone may be insufficient; making the governing architectures explicit may be necessary for diagnosing which disagreements are informational, which are architectural, and which are both. The IRIS method does not resolve disagreement. It makes the architecture of disagreement visible — enabling disputants to identify not merely what they disagree about but which processing rules generate the disagreement.

The method’s limitations should be stated alongside its claims. IRIS operates under a scope condition — signal constancy — that restricts its applicability to domains where the grammars under comparison share enough empirical substrate for the invariance claim to hold. It does not resolve normative questions about which grammar is preferable; it provides the architectural map on which such questions can be more precisely posed. Its validation evidence is preliminary and computationally derived; human-participant replication is required before stronger claims are warranted. And its reflexive apparatus, while conceptually rigorous, has not yet been tested under conditions where the analyst’s home grammar might systematically distort the rotation in ways that declaration alone cannot correct.

These limitations specify the instrument’s current range of application. The method is offered as a contribution to the growing body of work — from analytical pluralism in philosophy of science to multiverse analysis in quantitative methods to orders of worth in the sociology of justification — that takes seriously the proposition that the instruments of observation participate in producing the observations they deliver. What the IRIS method adds to this body of work is an explicit protocol: hold the input constant, declare the processing rules, rotate the grammar, and document what changes. The IRIS method proposes that the architecture of interpretation can be made visible, specified, and contested — and that making it so is an enabling condition for understanding why the same world produces such different conclusions.
