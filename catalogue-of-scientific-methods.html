Abstract

This catalogue maps the methods by which the sciences produce and test knowledge. It covers 157 works, from Aristotle’s account of demonstration and Ibn al-Haytham’s optics, through experimental medicine, statistics and causal inference, survey and case-study methods, to computer simulation, data-centred research, replication studies and participatory research. Each work has an entry drawn from a register shared with the Catalogue of Epistemological Science. The entry gives what the work says, a separate reading of it, and its position on six axes along which methods differ, where it takes one. The axes are: the primary source of evidence (observation or model-based), the standard of acceptance (testability or coherence), the bearer of rationality (the individual or the community), the place of values (value-neutral or situated), the level of explanation (components or organisation), and the aim of inquiry (representation or intervention). The aim is a map, not a ranking. The catalogue does not argue for one method. A reader can use it to see how a method answers each of the six questions, which questions it leaves aside, and which works answer it. The map involves substantive claims about how the works relate and about the register itself. Each statement in the section notes and the synthesis carries a status, and the main contested claims are set out, with their status and sources, in a claims ledger (§15). Two constructed test questions, on number words and arithmetic and on whether material accompanying a recording changes what a listener notices, illustrate how the axes separate approaches (§14.4); they report no findings. The selection is purposive and weighted toward the Western academic tradition, with entries on Islamic optics, Indigenous methodologies and participatory research; it is not an exhaustive global survey.

  • Entries sit in ten sections (§4–§13). Each entry has a register ID such as R-0009. The ID is permanent and is the same in both catalogues. Within a section, entries are ordered by year of first publication.
  • Each entry opens with a line giving the work’s tradition, period, type and claim type, as defined in the codebook.
  • Summary reports what the work says and is a checkable claim. Reading is the author’s interpretation and is marked as such. The two are kept apart throughout.
  • Axes give the work’s position on the six axes defined in §3, in their order there: source of evidence · standard of acceptance · bearer of rationality · place of values · level of explanation · aim of inquiry. Entries print the pole names; a work coded on both poles shows both (for example Observation and model-based), and n/a means the work does not address that axis’s question. The tables in §14 use A, B, X (both) and n/a. The reason for each code is recorded in the coding log, published with the register.
  • Links name related entries by ID and say how they relate: extends, critiques or answers. A link is a relation the register draws; it does not mean that the later work cites the earlier. Linked from names the entries whose links point to this one, with the relation.
  • First published, Edition used (only where it differs from the first) and Translated by give the record of the work; a Note, where present, adds a point about dating, translation or the record.
  • Each section ends with What this section adds, a note whose statements carry a status (§2).
  • C-01 and similar mark a statement in the claims ledger (§15). It is the ledger both catalogues draw on; the Catalogue of Epistemological Science holds the IDs not listed in §15. Q-A and Q-B are the two test questions used in the synthesis (§14.4).
  • Works that appear in both catalogues are marked [shared]. Their summary is the same in both. The other catalogue reads them from its own question.
  • Statuses (checkable claim, proposal, direction, question, illustration) and evidence grades (Strong, Moderate, Suggestive, Contested, Open) are defined in §2.
  • References to the codebook (for example “codebook, §6.2”) point to the codebook in the Register of Works, published in the same dataset.
  • The Index of codes, before the Glossary, lists every work under each code. Technical terms are defined in the Glossary at the end. Works cited only in the claims ledger are listed after the References, under “Additional sources cited in the claims ledger”.

In brief

This catalogue compares methods of scientific inquiry by their answers to six questions, which §3 defines as axes: where the decisive evidence comes from; what a claim must survive to be accepted; who is rational; whether values and the investigator’s position can be removed from method; at which level explanation stops; and whether success means accuracy or the ability to act. Classical accounts of induction and testing, statistics, causal inference, ethnography, simulation and participatory research in the register give different answers, and the works collected here describe several families of method rather than one (§14.2). Proposal. Many current disputes, over replication, over theories that cannot yet be tested, over models whose steps no one can fully survey, and over who research is accountable to, are disputes about those answers. The catalogue lays them out side by side so that a reader can see what is at stake when a method is chosen.

Five entries show the range of answers. Popper (R-0009) counts a theory as scientific if it forbids some possible observations, and holds it corroborated, never verified, while it survives severe attempts to refute it (Observation · Testability · Individual · Value-neutral · n/a · Representation). Kuhn (R-0013) describes normal science as puzzle-solving within a paradigm shared by a trained community, and revolutions in which the community adopts a new paradigm (Observation · Coherence · Social · n/a · n/a · Representation). Edwards (R-0093) describes global climate data that are themselves filtered by models, and models tested against those data (Observation and model-based · Testability and coherence · Social · n/a · n/a · Representation). Mitchell (R-0039) argues that complex biological systems need several models at different levels, integrated for particular problems rather than reduced to one theory (Observation and model-based · Testability and coherence · Individual · n/a · Organisation · Representation and intervention). Smith (R-0069) sets out research designed by and accountable to Indigenous communities, whose concepts and protocols set the terms of evidence (n/a · Coherence · Social · Situated · n/a · Intervention). The five are chosen for spread across the codes, not for rank; §14.1 counts every code, and each entry’s links lead to the works that answer it.

What the entries are. Each summary compresses a work into a few sentences: it is a guide to the work, not a substitute for reading it, and not a review of its secondary literature; the reading beside it is the author’s and is marked as such. The six axes are not new either: oppositions such as observation and model, testability and coherence, or individual and social recur throughout the philosophy of science. What the catalogue adds is their combination: a register shared with the Catalogue of Epistemological Science, six axes stated as questions and coded with their reasons, and a comparison of methods by the answers they give, applied across a broad but purposively selected set of works. It is a map, not a verdict. A code places a work on an axis by what its summary states, and a specialist may read a work differently. The coding log, published with the register, gives the reason for every code by register ID, so that each code can be checked and contested.

Framework · Section 1

The question

What methods do the sciences use to produce and test knowledge, and along which lines do those methods differ?

Proposal. The question has two parts. The first is descriptive: which methods are there? The second is comparative: what distinguishes them? A list alone answers the first part and leaves the second open. A single standard answers the second part by judging every method against one requirement: for example, that a claim be exposed to possible empirical refutation, which Popper proposed as the criterion that separates empirical science from other claims (R-0009; C-01). This catalogue is neither a list nor a single standard. It describes each method in its own terms and then places it on axes chosen to show differences rather than to rank. Question. Whether six axes are enough is left open: two methods that differ in practice but receive the same six codes would mark a difference the axes miss (§16).

Framework · Section 2

Scope, sources and method

Scope. The catalogue covers works that propose, analyse or criticise a method of scientific inquiry, or that study how methods work in practice. It includes the natural, social and human sciences, and methods of inquiry from outside the Western academic tradition, including knowledge practices that make claims about the natural world. It does not treat mathematics, the arts or law as fields of method. Formal works enter where they state a method or a result used in the sciences, as Shannon’s theory of communication does (R-0082); on axis 1, formal derivation counts as a model-based source of evidence.

Related resources. Checkable claim. Other open resources map philosophy or the philosophy of science by other means. The Indiana Philosophy Ontology (InPhO) builds a dynamic ontology of the discipline, starting from an encyclopedia and refined by expert feedback and crowdsourcing (Buckner, Niepert & Allen 2011; Eckert et al. 2010); Pasin and Motta (2011) set out an ontology for annotating philosophical resources; co-citation analysis has mapped representative epistemologists and philosophers of science by who is cited with whom (Kreuzman 2001); SOPHIS is an open database for quantitative studies of contemporary philosophy of science (Petrovich et al. 2026); the Comp HOPOS dataset gives metadata for the computational history of philosophy of science (Hicks, Morris & Brister 2019); and the PhilPapers Survey records the positions of professional philosophers on a set of questions (Bourget & Chalmers 2014). Proposal. This catalogue differs from them in coding works by hand on stated questions, with the reason for each code recorded, and in surveying works rather than people or citations, from antiquity to the 2020s and across several traditions. The comparison rests on the literature searched, not on a systematic review.

Selection. The selection is purposive, not systematic. Works were chosen because they state a method or a position on method that other works in the field answer to, or because they fill a gap that would otherwise leave a family of method unrepresented. The selection under-represents work not published in English or in English translation, and work from the last decade. §17 lists the main gaps that remain.

Register. Each work has one entry in a shared register. The entry records the first publication, the edition used for page references, the translator, a summary, a reading, codes and links to other entries. Bibliographic data were checked against publisher, journal, Crossref and library records; the state of the check for each work is recorded in the register and summarised in the provenance record published with the register. Where a page is cited, it refers to the edition named in the register.

Coding. Positions on the axes were coded by one coder, with AI assistance in applying the codebook (the provenance record describes it). The coder applied the codebook to each work’s argument as stated in its summary, decided every code, and checked that works taking the same position carry the same code across the register. Each code and the reason for it are recorded in the coding log, published with the register. An intra-rater check, in which a random sample is re-coded after an interval and agreement is reported, is specified in the intra-rater protocol published with the register, and its sample has been drawn; the re-coding has not been carried out, so no reliability statistics are reported. No second coder was used. §14.1 reports instead how the counts change if the works coded both are recoded to either pole, and Appendix A works through one borderline case.

Statuses. The opening paragraph of each section says what the section contains. Each statement in the section notes (What this section adds), in §14, in the claims ledger (§15), in the open questions (§16) and in Appendix A carries a status: checkable claim (a statement about what a work says or what a study found, which a reader can check against the source), proposal (an organising device or reading the author offers), direction (a line worth pursuing, not yet argued in full), question, or illustration (a constructed example that reports no finding). A qualifier after a status names what the statement is about, as in checkable claim (of the register): a statement about how the register is coded or what it counts, which a reader checks by regenerating it from the register rather than against the works, and which is not a finding about the field. A passage or claim with parts of different kinds gives each part its own status, in parentheses, as C-03 does (§15).

Evidence grades. Where a checkable claim concerns empirical evidence, it also carries a grade from the evidence protocol used across the thoughts papers (codebook, §6.2). Strong: at least three independent studies, including at least one within-person or experimentally manipulated comparison; effects in the predicted direction; no major published contradiction. Moderate: at least two independent studies, at least one of which partly addresses a named confound; the remaining confound is stated. Suggestive: one direct study, or converging indirect evidence; the missing control is stated. Contested: directly conflicting results, or an unresolved methodological dispute, which is named. Open: no direct test located, or the only direct test located is a null result; the prediction and a design that would test it are stated. In a graded claim the verb marks the grade, as the codebook sets out: shows (Strong), indicates (Moderate), suggests (Suggestive), conflict or is disputed (Contested), is untested (Open). A grade attaches to the link between a claim and its evidence, not to a study: where the text reports what a study found, it reports that study’s result, and the grade says how well the wider claim is supported. A qualifier in parentheses names the part of a claim that is graded, as in Contested (generalisation). Claims about interpretation or history, claims about what a work argues, and claims about the register’s own codes and counts are not claims about empirical evidence and carry no grade, marked “—”. In this version two claims carry a grade, both Contested: C-04 and C-05 (§13, §15).

What this catalogue is not. It is not a textbook of research design, and it does not recommend a method for any particular problem. It reports no new data.

Framework · Section 3

Six axes of method

Proposal. Methods differ along at least six axes. Each runs between two poles, and neither pole is better than the other. The table gives the codebook’s definition of each pole and the question a coder asks of each work; the codebook, published with the register, sets the rules for coding and gives examples.

# Axis: question to ask of the work Pole A Pole B
1 Primary source of evidence: where does the work say the decisive evidence comes from? Observation: data gathered from the world by observation or experiment Model-based: results generated by a constructed system (model, simulation or formal derivation) that stands in for the world
2 Standard of acceptance: what must a claim survive to be accepted? Testability: claims must be exposed to possible empirical failure Coherence: non-empirical standards can justify acceptance (five senses, below)
3 Bearer of rationality: who, on the work’s account, is rational, or fails to be? Individual: method is justified by the reasoning of an individual investigator Social: method is justified by the organisation and critical exchange of a community, or by a distributed arrangement of people, instruments and materials that no single investigator carries
4 Place of values: can values or the knower’s position be removed from method? Value-neutral: method can and should exclude social and ethical values from the assessment of evidence Situated: values or the knower’s position enter method, and accounting for them is part of objectivity
5 Level of explanation: at which level does explanation stop? Components: explanation stops at lower-level parts and the laws governing them (reduction) Organisation: explanation stops at higher-level patterns or organisation, with properties not derivable from the parts (emergence)
6 Aim of inquiry: is success measured by accuracy or by what can be done? Representation: success is an accurate description of the world Intervention: success is the capacity to act on the world (four senses, below)

Each work receives one code per axis: pole A, pole B, both where the work argues that the two poles must be combined or treats them as interdependent, or not applicable where it does not address the question. Not applicable is part of the result: it does not mean that the coder could not decide. Where a work argues for one pole and discusses the other, the pole it argues for is coded. Every code records what a work says or proposes, not the catalogue’s view. Appendix A works through one borderline case, and §14.1 shows how the counts change if every both is recoded as pole A or as pole B.

Five glosses matter. On axis 1 the question is the primary source of evidence, so a work that treats observation and models as interdependent is coded both. Pole B is called model-based to keep it apart from the social construction of facts discussed in §8, which is not coded on axis 1: the works that argue it are coded social on axis 3 and, where they describe acceptance as social closure, coherence on axis 2. On axis 2, coherence covers five non-empirical standards, which the codebook distinguishes and the coding log names for each work: holistic fit (Duhem, Quine), explanatory coherence or unification (Lipton, Kitcher), non-empirical assessment (Dawid), demonstration from premises held to be certain (Aristotle, Descartes) and social closure (Collins, Latour, Smith). Social closure records a work’s account of how claims come to be accepted; it is not an endorsement. The axis does not assume that a claim cannot be both testable and coherent: a work that requires both, as Bayesian works usually do, is coded both. On axis 4, situated covers two arguments: that the knower’s position must be accounted for (Haraway, Harding), and that values legitimately set how much evidence is enough (Rudner, Douglas). On axis 5, the code records where a work says explanation stops, not whether higher-level properties exist. On axis 6, intervention can be experimental manipulation (Hacking), a causal intervention (Woodward, Pearl), practical change in the situation studied (participatory research) or control of a system toward a goal (cybernetics); it is coded where intervention is the test or the aim of the work.

Checkable claim (of the register). The axes are not independent. Works coded social on axis 3 that take a position on axis 4 are mostly coded situated, yet most social works take no position on axis 4, and for every pair of axes but one some works take opposite poles; the exception, place of values with level of explanation, rests on few works (§14.1; C-06). Longino (R-0057) is social on axis 3 and situated on axis 4, but testable on axis 2. Merton (R-0049) is social on axis 3 and value-neutral on axis 4. Axes 2 and 3 also meet by definition: a work that describes acceptance as social closure is coded coherence on axis 2, and all but one of the works in the register that do so are coded social on axis 3; the exception, Lincoln and Guba (R-0249), makes the researcher the main instrument and is coded individual. The axes are kept separate because the works do not always move together on them.

Checkable claim (of the register). The axes describe arguments, not disciplines: Duhem, a physicist, is coded coherence on axis 2 (R-0007), and Merton, a sociologist, testability (R-0049).

Entries · Section 4

Inference, induction and the logic of testing

This section collects accounts of how evidence supports or refutes a claim: demonstration, induction, abduction, falsification and their critics. It runs from Aristotle and Ibn al-Haytham, through Bacon, Descartes, Galileo and Newton, and the nineteenth-century debate between Whewell and Mill, to the twentieth-century debates over falsification, over whether hypotheses can be tested one at a time, and over how a hypothesis is chosen when the evidence does not separate the alternatives. Chamberlin and Platt set out rules for testing rival hypotheses together, and Hanson gives an account of how hypotheses are found.

R-0113 · Aristotle (c. 350 BCE). Posterior Analytics [shared]

classical · 4th c. BCE · treatise · claim type: formal

Summary. Defines scientific knowledge (epistēmē) as knowledge of why a thing is so, through demonstration: a deduction from premises that are true, primary, immediate, better known than and prior to the conclusion, and causes of it (I.2, 71b20–22). First principles are not demonstrated but grasped by understanding (nous), reached from perception through induction (II.19).

Reading. Knowledge here belongs to one who grasps why a thing is so, and the chain of demonstration ends in principles that are understood, not proved. The account is structured as a model of a whole body of knowledge, which early modern works on method criticise (Bacon 1620, R-0002, and Galilei 1638, R-0189).

Axes: Observation · Coherence · Individual · n/a · n/a · Representation

Links. answers Plato c. 369 BCE, R-0112 in the Catalogue of Epistemological Science

Linked from. Bacon 1620, R-0002 (critiques); Galilei 1638, R-0189 (critiques); McGinnis 2010, R-0216 in the Catalogue of Epistemological Science (extends); Ibn Sīnā (Avicenna) c. 1020–1027, R-0235 in the Catalogue of Epistemological Science (extends)

First published: Composed as lecture material; conventional dating. Edition used: Barnes, J. (Trans.) (1994). Aristotle: Posterior Analytics (2nd ed.). Clarendon Press. Cited by Bekker number (71a–100b).

R-0001 · Ibn al-Haytham (c. 1011–1021). Kitāb al-Manāẓir (Book of Optics)

Islamic · 11th c. · treatise · claim type: methodological

Summary. Treats vision as the entry of light from objects into the eye and tests rival accounts of sight against controlled observations made with apertures, dark chambers and screens. The work judges between the accounts by what the arranged observations show, and repeats trials under varied conditions. Its introduction commits the inquirer to judge without prejudice and to seek the truth rather than be swayed by opinion (Book I, chapter 1).

Reading. An early case in this map of a method built around arranged observation (i’tibār) rather than authority. Its date bears on histories of method that begin in seventeenth-century Europe. It opens a question that Franklin (R-0019) takes up for modern experiment: what an arrangement must rule out before an observation can decide between accounts.

Axes: Observation · Testability · Individual · Value-neutral · n/a · Representation

Note. The Arabic term i’tibār is often translated as experiment; whether that translation fits is debated among historians of science. Sabra traces the concept to its use in astronomy: Sabra, A. I. (1971). The Astronomical Origin of Ibn al-Haytham’s Concept of Experiment. In Actes du XIIe Congrès International d’Histoire des Sciences (Paris, 1968), Vol. 3A, pp. 133–136. The summary covers Books I–III, the books translated in the edition used.

First published: Composed in Cairo; circulated in manuscript and in Latin as De aspectibus. Edition used: Sabra, A. I. (Trans.) (1989). The Optics of Ibn al-Haytham, Books I–III: On Direct Vision (2 vols.). Warburg Institute.

R-0002 · Bacon, F. (1620). Novum Organum

empiricist · 17th c. · book · claim type: methodological

Summary. Proposes a reformed induction that proceeds from collected and tabulated instances (of presence, absence and degree) to intermediate axioms, and warns against four kinds of mental error, the idols. Book II works the tables through for the form of heat and seeks the hidden processes and structures of bodies. Bacon pairs the power to produce effects with obedience to nature: nature is conquered only by being obeyed (Book I, aphorism 3).

Reading. Method here is a discipline of the investigator as much as a procedure, and much of the work is about guarding inquiry against its own habits. The reading of the work as a programme for dominating nature is contested (see C-03).

R-0188 · Descartes, R. (1637). Discours de la méthode (Discourse on the Method)

rationalist · 17th c. · book · claim type: methodological

Summary. Sets out four rules of method: accept nothing as true that is not clearly and distinctly known, avoiding precipitate conclusions and preconceptions; divide each difficulty into as many parts as possible; proceed in order from the simplest to the most complex; and make enumerations so complete that nothing is omitted. Published as the preface to essays on optics, meteorology and geometry that apply the method. Part VI gives experiments a subordinate task: deciding among the ways in which particular effects can follow from principles already established by reasoning.

Reading. The deductive counterpart to Bacon’s induction, in which method begins from certainty and analysis rather than from collected instances.

Axes: Model-based · Coherence · Individual · Value-neutral · Components · Representation

First published: Leiden: Jan Maire. Edition used: Cottingham, J., Stoothoff, R., & Murdoch, D. (Trans.) (1985). The Philosophical Writings of Descartes, Vol. I. Cambridge University Press. Cited by AT VI page.

R-0189 · Galilei, G. (1638). Discorsi e dimostrazioni matematiche intorno a due nuove scienze (Two New Sciences)

classical · 17th c. · book · claim type: formal

Summary. Presents two new mathematical sciences, of the strength of materials and of motion. Derives the law of falling bodies from definitions and postulates and checks it against measurements with inclined planes, combining geometrical demonstration with idealised experiment.

Reading. The union of mathematical idealisation and experimental check that later accounts of method take as the model of physics. Feyerabend’s account of Galileo (§5, R-0016) concerns the defence of Copernicanism rather than this work, and gives a contrasting account of how Galileo’s claims won acceptance.

Axes: Observation and model-based · Testability · Individual · n/a · n/a · Representation

Links. critiques Aristotle c. 350 BCE, R-0113

First published: Leiden: Elzevir. Edition used: Drake, S. (Trans.) (1974). Two New Sciences. University of Wisconsin Press.

R-0003 · Newton, I. (1687). Philosophiæ Naturalis Principia Mathematica

classical · 17th c. · book · claim type: methodological

Summary. Book III opens with rules for reasoning in natural philosophy: admit no more causes than are true and sufficient; assign the same causes to the same effects; treat qualities found in all bodies within reach of experiment as universal; and hold propositions gathered by induction as true until other phenomena make them more exact or liable to exceptions. The commentary on Rule III argues that the extension, hardness, impenetrability, mobility and inertia of the whole arise from those of the parts. The General Scholium, added in 1713, states that Newton frames no hypotheses about the cause of gravity.

Paradigms, programmes and the history of practice

These works read method from the history of science and of scientific practice. They ask how communities have in fact accepted, defended and abandoned theories, how experiments and instruments come to be trusted, how ideals of objectivity have changed, and what follows for any account of method.

R-0013 · Kuhn, T. S. (1962). The Structure of Scientific Revolutions [shared]

historical · 1960s · book · claim type: cultural

R-0014 · Lakatos, I. (1970). Falsification and the Methodology of Scientific Research Programmes

Summary. These works set out the design of experiments and the quantitative treatment of inference, and differ on what a statistical result establishes: experimental medicine, randomised design, significance and hypothesis testing, Bayesian updating, error statistics, iterative model-building, two frameworks for causal inference, causal judgement from observed associations, laboratory experiments in economics, the synthesis of many studies, and evidence-based practice.

R-0194 · Bernard, C. (1865). Introduction à l’étude de la médecine expérimentale (An Introduction to the Study of Experimental Medicine)

empiricist · 19th c. · book · claim type: methodological

Summary. Sets out the experimental method in physiology and medicine: an observation suggests an idea, the idea is tested by an experiment designed to verify or refute it, and comparative experiments with controls isolate the cause. Defends the determinism of physiological phenomena under controlled conditions. Requires a counterproof: once the supposed cause is removed, the effect must no longer appear (Part I, ch. II, §VIII). Rejects averages as expressions of physiological reality: mixing a man’s urine over twenty-four hours gives ‘an analysis of a urine which simply does not exist’ (p. 134).

Reading. Makes the controlled comparison, rather than the accumulation of clinical observations, the step that turns an idea into knowledge in medicine, between Mill’s methods and Fisher’s randomisation.

R-0024 · Neyman, J., & Pearson, E. S. (1933). On the Problem of the Most Efficient Tests of Statistical Hypotheses

computational-statistical · 1930s · article · claim type: formal

Summary. Frames hypothesis testing as a decision between a hypothesis and an alternative, with two kinds of error (rejecting a true hypothesis, accepting a false one). The fundamental lemma identifies the most powerful test for a given rate of the first error: for two simple hypotheses, the test that rejects when the ratio of their likelihoods is large has, among all tests with that rate, the greatest chance of rejecting the hypothesis when the alternative is true.

Reading. Testing becomes a rule of behaviour with known long-run error rates rather than a measure of belief. Later entries take up that choice (Mayo 1996, R-0028; Wasserstein and Lazar 2016, R-0201).

R-0025 · Fisher, R. A. (1935). The Design of Experiments

computational-statistical · 1930s · book · claim type: methodological

Summary. Sets out the principles of randomisation, replication and blocking in the design of experiments, and the logic of the test of significance against a null hypothesis, illustrated by the lady tasting tea. Randomisation is what makes a valid test of significance possible. In the tea-tasting case a lady is given eight cups, four with the milk poured first, in random order; if she cannot discriminate, she will pick the right four by chance once in 70 trials. Every experiment, on Fisher’s account, exists to give the facts a chance of disproving the null hypothesis, which is never proved (ch. II).

Reading. Makes randomisation the condition of a valid test, and so ties the design of an experiment to what its analysis can show. Later work on iterative model-building, trials and causal inference builds on it (Box 1976, R-0247; Imbens and Rubin 2015, R-0198).

R-0196 · Hill, A. B. (1965). The Environment and Disease: Association or Causation?

computational-statistical · 1960s · article · claim type: methodological

Summary. Proposes nine viewpoints for judging whether an observed association between a feature of the environment and a disease is causal (strength, consistency, specificity, temporality, biological gradient, plausibility, coherence, experiment and analogy), and holds that none of them can be required as a sine qua non. Its examples include cigarette smoking and cancer of the lung, where the death rate among cigarette smokers was nine to ten times that among non-smokers. Treats tests of significance as a servant of this judgement, not its master. Argues that the strength of evidence required before acting depends on the consequences of the action: slight evidence might justify restricting a drug for morning sickness in pregnancy, while asking people to change their fuel or diet needs strong evidence.

Reading. Moves causal judgement from a single test to a weighing of several considerations, and ties the evidence needed to what is at stake in acting on it.

R-0247 · Box, G. E. P. (1976). Science and Statistics

computational-statistical · 1970s · article · claim type: methodological

Summary. Describes scientific method as a motivated iteration in which practice confronts theory, and theory, practice. Rapid progress needs the flexibility to learn from these confrontations, parsimonious but effective models, selective attention to a model’s inadequacies, and mathematics used skilfully but appropriately. Every model is wrong, so the scientist’s task is to find which of its faults matter. Fisher’s development of statistical methods at Rothamsted is the paper’s illustration.

Reading. Treats design and analysis as stages of one learning cycle rather than as a single decisive test, which sets Fisher’s work (R-0025) in a sequence of experiments.

R-0200 · Smith, V. L. (1982). Microeconomic Systems as an Experimental Science

computational-statistical · 1980s · article · claim type: methodological

Summary. Sets out the methodology of laboratory experiments in economics: induced-value theory, by which monetary rewards give subjects controlled preferences, and the conditions under which laboratory markets can test economic theories. Lists the conditions a reward scheme must meet, among them nonsatiation, saliency, dominance and privacy, and holds that results carry over to markets outside the laboratory where similar conditions hold (parallelism).

Reading. A method of experiment adapted to a science long thought to be observational only.

R-0197 · Evidence-Based Medicine Working Group (1992). Evidence-Based Medicine: A New Approach to Teaching the Practice of Medicine

computational-statistical · 1990s · article · claim type: methodological

Summary. Proposes a new paradigm for medical practice that de-emphasises intuition, unsystematic clinical experience and pathophysiological reasoning as grounds for decisions, and emphasises the examination of evidence from clinical research. It requires new skills of the physician: efficient searching of the literature and the application of formal rules of evidence to appraise it. The paper describes a residency programme built to teach this practice.

Reading. The randomised trial as a standard of evidence in clinical practice, with the appraisal of research placed in the hands of the individual physician.

R-0029 · Spirtes, P., Glymour, C., & Scheines, R. (1993). Causation, Prediction, and Search

computational-statistical · 1990s · book · claim type: computational

Summary. Develops algorithms for inferring causal structure, represented as directed graphs, from patterns of statistical independence in observational data, under stated assumptions such as the causal Markov and faithfulness conditions. The Markov condition says that, once its direct causes are fixed, a variable is independent of every variable that is not among its effects; faithfulness says that the data show no independences beyond those the graph entails. From an inferred structure the book predicts the effects of manipulating a variable (the Manipulation Theorem, section 3.7.2 of the book).

Reading. Turns causal discovery into a computational procedure whose assumptions are stated explicitly.

R-0028 · Mayo, D. G. (1996). Error and the Growth of Experimental Knowledge

computational-statistical · 1990s · book · claim type: formal

Summary. Defends error statistics: a hypothesis is supported only if it passes a severe test, one it would probably have failed if it were false. Knowledge grows by learning from error through piecemeal, local tests rather than by appraising whole theories. Chapter 7 works the account through the experiments on Brownian motion, and chapters 9 to 11 set it against Neyman–Pearson and Bayesian readings of statistics.

Reading. A reconstruction of Popper in statistical terms, and a frequentist alternative to the Bayesian accounts in this map.

R-0030 · Pearl, J. (2000). Causality: Models, Reasoning, and Inference

computational-statistical · 2000s · book · claim type: formal

Summary. Provides a formal language for causal claims (structural causal models, causal graphs and the do-operator) that distinguishes seeing from doing and states when causal effects can be identified from observational data. Treats counterfactuals within the same framework. Causal conclusions follow only from data combined with causal assumptions encoded in a model; data alone do not settle them. The expression P(y | do(x)) gives the distribution of y when x is set by an intervention, which can differ from P(y | x), the distribution of y among cases observed to have x.

Reading. Makes intervention a formal object of method, so the representation–intervention axis can be stated precisely.

R-0026 · Jaynes, E. T. (2003). Probability Theory: The Logic of Science

computational-statistical · 2000s · book · claim type: formal

Summary. Presents probability theory as the extension of deductive logic to reasoning with incomplete information, derived from requirements of consistency. Defends Bayesian inference and the principle of maximum entropy for assigning prior probabilities: among the distributions consistent with what is known, choose the one that assumes least beyond it.

Reading. Method becomes the consistent updating of degrees of plausibility, which turns Popper’s yes-or-no test into a matter of degree.

R-0198 · Imbens, G. W., & Rubin, D. B. (2015). Causal Inference for Statistics, Social, and Biomedical Sciences: An Introduction

computational-statistical · 2010s · book · claim type: formal

Summary. Sets out the potential-outcomes framework for causal inference: a causal effect is the comparison of the outcomes a unit would have under different treatments, of which only one is observed. Treats randomised experiments, the assignment mechanism and the analysis of observational studies within this framework. It begins with classical randomised experiments, analysed by Fisher’s exact p-values and Neyman’s repeated-sampling approach, and treats observational studies under unconfounded assignment, designing them with the propensity score, matching and trimming apart from the analysis of outcomes (Parts II–IV).

Reading. An alternative to Pearl’s graphs for stating causal claims precisely; the two frameworks are largely translatable but foreground different assumptions.

R-0201 · Wasserstein, R. L., & Lazar, N. A. (2016). The ASA Statement on p-Values: Context, Process, and Purpose

computational-statistical · 2010s · article · claim type: methodological

Summary. The American Statistical Association’s statement of six principles on p-values, including that a p-value does not measure the probability that a hypothesis is true, that scientific conclusions should not rest only on whether a p-value passes a threshold, and that proper inference requires full reporting and transparency. It describes a p-value as the probability, under a specified statistical model, that a summary of the data would be equal to or more extreme than its observed value.

Reading. The institutional moment of the significance-testing debate, issued by a professional association of statisticians.

What this section adds. Checkable claim. The section contains at least four accounts of what a statistical result means: a test of significance, which gives the facts a chance of disproving a null hypothesis but never proves it (R-0025, R-0195); a long-run error rate for a decision rule (R-0024); a degree of belief, kept consistent by requirements of logic (R-0026) or of coherent decision (R-0027); and the severity of a test (R-0028). Checkable claim. Two formal frameworks for causal inference sit side by side: causal graphs (R-0030, R-0029) and potential outcomes (R-0198). Both make intervention a formal object, and both state when a causal effect can be estimated from observational data. They state their assumptions in different terms: as independences that a graph entails, or as conditions on how treatment was assigned. Proposal. Read in sequence, Bernard’s controlled comparison (R-0194), Fisher’s randomisation (R-0025) and evidence-based medicine (R-0197) show how the controlled, and then the randomised, comparison came to be treated as a standard of evidence in medicine. Checkable claim. Bernard himself rejected statistical averages in physiology, because an average can describe no actual case (R-0194). Hill’s viewpoints (R-0196) set out what to weigh before reading an observed association as causal, and count experiment as one of them. Box (R-0247) describes design and analysis as stages of an iteration between theory and practice, Glass (R-0031) combines the results of many studies, and Smith (R-0200) carries controlled experiment into economics. Checkable claim. Mayo (R-0199) applies severity to current disputes over significance tests and replication, and the ASA statement (R-0201) sets out what a p-value does and does not measure. Question. When a study draws on more than one of these accounts without saying so, which account fixes what its result means?

Entries · Section 7

Explanation, laws and models

These works ask what a scientific explanation is, what laws are, how models represent, whether method aims at truth or at empirical adequacy, and what part aesthetic criteria play in choosing theories.

R-0183 · Whitehead, A. N. (1925). Science and the Modern World [shared]

process · 1920s · book · claim type: cultural

Summary. Lowell Lectures on the history of scientific thought. Argues that seventeenth-century science rested on scientific materialism, which mistakes abstractions such as simple location for concrete reality: the ‘Fallacy of Misplaced Concreteness’ (ch. III, p. 51 in the 1967 edition). In its place the lectures set out an organic philosophy of events constituted by their relations, and describe philosophy as ‘the critic of abstractions’, which harmonises them and completes them by comparison with more concrete intuitions (ch. V).

Reading. Reads the fallacy as a risk in any method that works with abstractions, and moves what is concrete from located matter to events in their relations.

R-0046 · Wigner, E. P. (1960). The Unreasonable Effectiveness of Mathematics in the Natural Sciences

logical-analytic · 1960s · article · claim type: conceptual

Summary. Observes that mathematical concepts, chosen by mathematicians for the scope they give to ingenious and elegant manipulation, turn up in unexpected connections and often describe phenomena with unexpected accuracy, and argues that there is no rational explanation for this usefulness. Asks whether, for that reason, a physical theory can be known to be uniquely appropriate. Its opening story is of a statistician whose paper on population trends contains the symbol π, which a classmate takes for a joke.

Reading. States a puzzle rather than a method: the fit between mathematics and nature is left unexplained, and what follows is a question about the uniqueness of physical theories. Whitehead (R-0183) asks the neighbouring question of what successful abstractions do not show.

R-0041 · Simon, H. A. (1969). The Sciences of the Artificial

systems-cybernetic · 1960s · book · claim type: computational

Summary. Proposes sciences of design, concerned with how things ought to be in order to attain goals, alongside the natural sciences. Sets out bounded rationality and satisficing, which Simon introduced in the 1950s, and argues that complex systems are often nearly decomposable hierarchies, in which interactions within subsystems are much stronger than those between them; the parable of two watchmakers, Hora and Tempus, shows why such systems come about more readily. Treats simulation as a source of new knowledge, even about systems whose laws are known (ch. 1).

Reading. A source for treating design as a method of inquiry in its own right. Near decomposability bears on the level of explanation: where subsystems interact weakly, a system can be explained part by part and by its organisation.

In the last column, A and B are the poles of each axis as defined in §3. The rows are methods drawn from the families of §14.2: comparison from induction and comparison; the randomised experiment and causal graphs from probabilistic and causal inference; severe testing from the logic of testing; the survey from surveys; case study, ethnography and grounded theory from the interpretive and case-based family; modelling from explanation and modelling; situated and participatory research from the participatory and Indigenous family and from values and situated knowledge; and synthesis, which belongs to no single family. Comparison, randomised experiment and severe testing hold other factors fixed or balanced so that one factor can be examined; their answer concerns that factor under the conditions of the design. A survey describes how the outcome is distributed in a population. Case study, ethnography, grounded theory and participatory design keep the setting in view; their answer concerns how the factor works there, and how far it extends is a further question. Causal graphs, modelling and synthesis state or combine what the other methods assume or find. Which method a study uses follows from which part of the question it asks, and that choice can be stated in terms of the axes.

14.5 Method as a constraint on what can be found

Direction. A method can be described by what it makes visible and what it excludes: a randomised design excludes variation it cannot assign; an ethnography excludes what it cannot observe in place; a simulation excludes what its model leaves out (Whitehead, R-0183, warns against mistaking such abstractions for the world). The last column of §14.2 records the same point family by family. Checkable claim. Ashby gives constraint a general sense: it exists when the variety under one condition is less than the variety under another, and every law of nature is a constraint (R-0083, S.7/8, S.7/15). This catalogue does not develop the point into a theory. It records it as a direction: a method can be described by what it constrains, and comparing methods means comparing their constraints (C-09).

Questions.

Section 17

Limitations and closing

Limitations.

  • Selection. The selection is purposive, made by one person, and weighted toward English-language and translated work and toward work before the last decade. Methods from Chinese, Indian, African and Latin American scientific traditions have few entries, which is not proportionate to their literatures. The table below lists the main gaps, each with a representative work, ranked by the author’s estimate of how much their inclusion would change the counts in §14.1; the estimate is not computed.
  • Contributors. The register was coded and its summaries drafted by one person, and it leans toward the Western academic tradition. Its depth, its coverage and the reliability of its codes would all gain from more contributors, above all specialists in the traditions it under-represents. Proposals of works, summaries and codes are invited.
  • Weighting. Each work counts once in §14.1, whatever its length or influence: a book and a short article weigh the same.
  • Summaries. Each summary compresses a work into a few sentences. It is a guide to the work, not a substitute for it. Summaries and readings were drafted with AI assistance from the works and the standard literature on them, then checked (provenance record; Declarations). A summary quotes a work only where the wording matters, with a page or another locator (chapter, essay, standard reference) in the edition named in the register; terms of art, such as virtual witnessing or trading zone, are used as terms, with the work that introduced them.
  • References. Most references are checked against the standard bibliographic record; the state of the check for each work is recorded in the register and counted in the provenance record.
  • Coding. Codes are the decisions of one coder; no second coder was used, and the intra-rater check, whose sample is drawn, has not been carried out. Counts in §14.1 describe the register as coded. Links between entries (extends, critiques, answers) are also one coder’s decisions, and the intra-rater protocol samples codes, not links.
  • Readings. Readings are the author’s and are marked as such. Other readings of every work exist.
  • No new data. The catalogue reports no new data.

Glossary

abduction
Inference to a hypothesis that would, if true, explain a surprising fact; the first stage of inquiry, before deduction and induction. See Peirce 1903, R-0006; Lipton 1991, R-0012.

agential realism
The view that phenomena are produced by intra-actions of apparatus and object, so that what becomes determinate depends on the arrangement of the inquiry. See Barad 2007, R-0067.

algorithmic modelling
Modelling that treats the data-generating mechanism as unknown and judges models by predictive accuracy. See Breiman 2001, R-0212.

analytic generalisation
Generalising from a case to a theory rather than from a sample to a population. See Yin 1984, R-0078.

autopoiesis
The organisation of a living system as a network of processes that produce the components that produce it. See Maturana & Varela 1980, R-0085.

auxiliary hypothesis
An assumption, for example about instruments, used together with a hypothesis to derive a testable prediction. See Duhem 1906, R-0007; Lakatos 1970, R-0014.

Bayesian inference
Updating degrees of belief in hypotheses on evidence by Bayes’s theorem. See Jaynes 2003, R-0026; Bernardo & Smith 1994, R-0027.

black box
A system studied only through the relation between its inputs and outputs; in science studies, a claim or device no longer opened to question. See Ashby 1956, R-0083; Latour 1987, R-0146.

bootstrap confirmation
Confirmation in which parts of a theory are used to compute values from data that then test other parts of the same theory. See Glymour 1980, R-0011.

co-production
The idea that ways of knowing the world and ways of ordering society are produced together. See Jasanoff 2004, R-0059.

coherence
Mutual support among claims within a system, used as a standard of acceptance alongside or instead of empirical test. As the name of pole B of axis 2 in the Catalogue of Scientific Methods, it covers five non-empirical standards, including demonstration and social closure (§3 of that catalogue). See Quine 1951, R-0008; Dawid 2013, R-0096.

colligation
Bringing facts together under a new conception supplied by the mind, which Whewell takes to be what induction does. See Whewell 1840, R-0005.

consilience of inductions
The case in which a hypothesis formed to explain one class of facts also explains a different class. See Whewell 1840, R-0005.

constructive empiricism
The view that science aims at empirical adequacy, truth about what is observable, not truth about the unobservable. See van Fraassen 1980, R-0042.

data models
Data that have been processed by models, as global climate data are, so that models are tested against data that themselves depend on modelling. See Edwards 2010, R-0093.

demarcation
The problem of distinguishing science from other kinds of claim. See Popper 1934, R-0009.

distributed cognition
Cognition carried out by a system of people, artefacts and procedures rather than by one mind. See Hutchins 1995, R-0080.

emergence
The appearance of properties at a level of organisation that are not derivable from the properties of components alone. See Kauffman 1993, R-0087; Mitchell 2003, R-0039.

entity realism
Realism about entities that can be used to intervene on other things, independent of realism about theories. See Hacking 1983, R-0018.

epistemic iteration
Building standards in successive stages, each correcting the last, without a prior theory to fix them. See Chang 2004, R-0021.

epistemic opacity
The condition of a process whose epistemically relevant elements an agent does not all know; essential opacity when the agent cannot know them. See Humphreys 2009, R-0092.

error statistics
An approach in which a hypothesis is supported only by passing tests that it would probably have failed if it were false (severe tests). See Mayo 1996, R-0028.

ethnomethodology
The study of the methods people use to produce and recognise social order in everyday activity. See Garfinkel 1967, R-0208.

evidence-based medicine
Clinical practice grounded in the systematic use of research evidence, appraised by formal rules of evidence. See Evidence-Based Medicine Working Group 1992, R-0197.

experimenter’s regress
The circularity in which a competent experiment is identified by its result, and the right result by competent experiments. See Collins 1985, R-0051.

explanatory unification
The view that to explain is to derive many phenomena from few argument patterns. See Kitcher 1981, R-0202.

fallacy of misplaced concreteness
Mistaking an abstraction for concrete reality. See Whitehead 1925, R-0183.

falsifiability
The property of a theory that some possible observation would contradict it. See Popper 1934, R-0009.

grounded theory
A method for generating theory from data by constant comparison and theoretical sampling. See Glaser & Strauss 1967, R-0076.

hard core and protective belt
In a research programme, the central assumptions held fixed and the auxiliary hypotheses adjusted to protect them. See Lakatos 1970, R-0014.

holism (Duhem–Quine)
The thesis that hypotheses are tested together with auxiliary assumptions, so a failed prediction does not show which is false. See Duhem 1906, R-0007; Quine 1951, R-0008.

incommensurability
The lack of a common measure between successive paradigms. See Kuhn 1962, R-0013.

induced-value theory
The use of monetary rewards to give laboratory subjects controlled preferences, so that economic theories can be tested in the laboratory. See Smith 1982, R-0200.

inductive risk
The risk of error in accepting or rejecting a hypothesis on incomplete evidence, which makes the consequences of error relevant to how much evidence is enough. See Rudner 1953, R-0206; Douglas 2009, R-0207.

inference to the best explanation
Inferring the hypothesis that would best explain the evidence. See Lipton 1991, R-0012.

interactional expertise
Fluency in a field’s language without the ability to practise it. See Collins & Evans 2007, R-0052.

intervention
In causal inference, setting a variable’s value from outside the system; the basis of interventionist accounts of causation. As the name of pole B of axis 6 in the Catalogue of Scientific Methods, it also covers experimental manipulation and practical change in the situation studied (§3 of that catalogue). See Woodward 2003, R-0033; Pearl 2000, R-0030.

kairetic account
An account of explanation on which an explanation picks out the difference-makers in a causal model, obtained by removing every detail that does not affect the outcome. See Strevens 2008, R-0034.

Kaupapa Māori research
Research designed by and for Māori communities, accountable to them and framed by Māori concepts. See Smith 1999, R-0069.

mechanical objectivity
The ideal of removing the observer’s intervention from the production of scientific images and data. See Daston & Galison 1992, R-0167; Daston & Galison 2007, R-0168.

mechanism
In mechanist accounts of explanation, entities and activities organised so that they produce a phenomenon. See Machamer et al. 2000, R-0245; Bechtel & Abrahamsen 2005, R-0246.

meta-analysis
The statistical synthesis of results from many studies. See Glass 1976, R-0031.

models as mediators
The view that models are partly independent of theory and data and mediate between them as instruments of inquiry. See Morgan & Morrison 1999, R-0203.

nomological machine
In Cartwright, a stable arrangement of components with stable capacities that, operated repeatedly in a suitable environment, gives rise to the regularity a law describes. See Cartwright 1999, R-0036.

non-empirical theory assessment
Support for a theory that does not come from testing its predictions, such as the absence of alternatives, unexpected explanatory coherence and the success of similar theories. See Dawid 2013, R-0096.

normal science
Puzzle-solving research within an accepted paradigm. See Kuhn 1962, R-0013.

paradigm
The shared exemplars, problems and standards of a scientific community. See Kuhn 1962, R-0013.

participatory action research
Research in which researchers and communities investigate together in order to act on the situation studied. See Fals-Borda & Rahman 1991, R-0073.

potential outcomes
A framework in which a causal effect compares the outcomes a unit would have under different treatments, only one of which is observed. See Imbens & Rubin 2015, R-0198.

preregistration
Specifying hypotheses and analyses before seeing the data, to separate confirmatory from exploratory research. See Nosek et al. 2018, R-0214.

process tracing
Within-case analysis of a causal mechanism through the sequence of events in a case. See George & Bennett 2005, R-0210.

randomisation
Assigning treatments by chance, so that a test of significance is valid and unmeasured differences are balanced in expectation. See Fisher 1935, R-0025.

research programme
A sequence of theories sharing a hard core, appraised as progressive or degenerating. See Lakatos 1970, R-0014.

robustness
Agreement of independent means of detection, taken as a mark that a result is real. See Wimsatt 2007, R-0040.

second-order cybernetics
The cybernetics of observing systems, which includes the observer in what is described. See von Foerster 2003, R-0086.

severe test
A test that a hypothesis would probably have failed if it were false. See Mayo 1996, R-0028.

situated knowledges
Knowledge that is partial, embodied and accountable for its location, offered as a form of objectivity. See Haraway 1988, R-0062.

strong inference
Systematically devising alternative hypotheses and crucial experiments to exclude them. See Platt 1964, R-0193.

strong objectivity
Objectivity that includes critical scrutiny of the researcher’s own background assumptions, starting from marginalised lives. See Harding 1991, R-0064.

thick description
Ethnographic description that records the layered meanings an action has for those who perform it. See Geertz 1973, R-0077.

total survey error
A framework that classifies the errors of survey estimates: coverage, sampling, non-response, measurement and processing. See Groves et al. 2004, R-0209.

trading zone
A setting in which subcultures of science coordinate their work through simplified shared languages without full agreement on meaning. See Galison 1997, R-0205.

Two-Eyed Seeing
Learning to see with the strengths of Indigenous knowledges through one eye and of mainstream knowledges through the other, and to use both together. See Bartlett et al. 2012, R-0072.

underdetermination
The situation in which evidence does not by itself settle which of several theories to accept. See Duhem 1906, R-0007; Quine 1951, R-0008.

value-free ideal
The view that values other than epistemic ones should play no part in the internal reasoning of science, such as deciding whether the evidence is enough to accept a claim. See Douglas 2009, R-0207.

view from nowhere
A conception of the world reached by stepping back from one’s particular perspective toward one that includes oneself. Nagel defends it while recording what it loses; Haraway rejects it as a ‘god trick’. See Nagel 1986, R-0139; Haraway 1988, R-0062.

virtual witnessing
Producing in readers, through detailed reports, the image of an experimental scene so that they can assent to its result as if present. See Shapin & Schaffer 1985, R-0204.
