Abstract

This catalogue maps theories of knowledge around three questions: where is knowledge located, who counts as a knower, and how are rival claims decided? It covers 125 works, from Plato’s Theaetetus, Aristotle, Nyāya and Buddhist theories of the means of knowledge, Avicenna, al-Ghazālī, Ibn Khaldūn and classical Chinese disputation, through early modern and pragmatist epistemology, to analytic (including post-Gettier, virtue, knowledge-first and social epistemology), phenomenological, feminist, Africana, decolonial, African, Indigenous, practice-based, enactive, systems-theoretical, process and extended accounts. Each work has an entry drawn from a register shared with the Catalogue of Scientific Methods. The entry gives what the work says, a separate reading of it, and its answers to the three questions. The aim is a map, not a verdict. The catalogue does not defend one theory of knowledge. It records how the theories in the register answer the three questions differently (§13.1 gives the counts), sets out the tensions between those answers, and proposes five composite modes for reading a disagreement from several positions at once, worked through on one constructed case of rival readings of the same evidence (§13.5). The map involves substantive claims about how the works relate. Each statement in the section notes and the synthesis carries a status, and the main contested claims are set out, with their status and sources, in a claims ledger (§14). The selection is purposive and weighted toward the Western academic tradition, with entries from Indian, Islamic, Chinese, African, Latin American and Indigenous traditions; it is not an exhaustive global survey.

In brief

This catalogue compares theories of knowledge by their answers to three questions rather than by their definitions of knowledge (§1 gives the reason). Where is knowledge: in a mind, in experience, in language, in a body, in a practice, in a community, in an institution, in a network of people and things? Who counts as a knower: any rational individual, a trained expert, a community, people in particular social positions, or non-human beings too? And when claims conflict, what decides between them: proof, test, probability, coherence, consensus, criticism, dialogue among positions that stay different, what works in practice, the consequences of holding a belief, or power? Classical, analytic, pragmatist, phenomenological, sociological, feminist, Africana, decolonial, African, Indigenous, systems and process theories in the register give different answers to each question, and for most codes a work’s answer to one question does not fix its answers to the others (C-11). The catalogue lays them out side by side and, in §13, proposes a way of reading one disagreement from several positions at once.

Four entries show the range of answers. Descartes (R-0119) places knowledge in the individual mind, counts any rational individual as a knower and decides by proof from a first certainty (mind | individual | proof). Dharmakīrti, in Dunne’s study (R-0115), grounds knowledge in perception and inference and judges a cognition by whether it leads to successful action (experience | individual | pragmatic). Hutchins (R-0080) finds a ship’s position computed by a system of people, instruments, charts and procedures (system | collective | practice). Santos (R-0165) keeps several knowledges and kinds of knower distinct and relates them by intercultural translation (plural | plural | dialogue). The four are chosen for spread across the codes, not for rank; §13.1 counts every answer.

What the entries are. Each summary compresses a work into a few sentences: it is a guide to the work, not a substitute for reading it, and not a review of its secondary literature; the reading beside it is the author’s and is marked as such. The three questions are not new: they overlap with long-standing debates about where knowledge is located, who knows, and how disagreements are resolved. What the catalogue adds is their combination: a register shared with the Catalogue of Scientific Methods, a coding scheme of three questions and a way of reading disagreement, applied across a broad but purposively selected set of traditions. It is a map, not a verdict. A code reduces a work to one answer to each question, and a specialist may read a work differently. The coding log, published with the register, gives the reason for every code by register ID, so that each code can be checked and contested.

Framework · Section 1

The three questions

  1. Where is knowledge located?
  2. Who counts as a knower?
  3. How are rival claims decided when there is no single, agreed foundation?

Proposal. The questions are chosen because every theory in the register answers them, explicitly or not, and because the answers can be compared. Definitions of knowledge, by contrast, are framed so differently across traditions that comparing them often means translating one tradition into another’s terms first. The three questions are meant to avoid much of that; whether they do is not tested here.

The questions overlap at their edges, and the catalogue keeps them apart by what each asks for. Location asks what carries or produces knowledge, or from what position it is held: a medium, an arrangement or a position. Knower asks whose standing as a knower a theory recognises: which agents, and on what terms. Decision asks what settles a disagreement: a procedure, or what a work describes as settling it, such as agreement or power. Illustration. A theory could locate knowledge in a community while treating any member as an interchangeable knower, and settle disputes by test. Checkable claim. In the register, Hountondji (R-0160) locates philosophy in a pluralist debate among philosophers, treats its practitioners as individual critical thinkers and settles claims by critique.

The third question is phrased so as not to assume that there is no foundation. It asks what decides when the parties to a disagreement do not share one, which is the usual situation in practice even for theories that hold that a foundation exists.

Framework · Section 2

Scope, sources and method

Scope. The catalogue covers works that propose, analyse or criticise an account of knowledge, of the knower, or of how claims are justified or decided, in philosophy and in neighbouring fields that make such accounts explicit (sociology of knowledge, science studies, feminist theory, decolonial thought, cognitive science, systems theory). Works whose main claim is a method of scientific inquiry are in the Catalogue of Scientific Methods. A work whose main claim concerns knowledge, the knower or how claims are decided stays here even where it also sets out a method (Peirce, R-0126; Dewey, R-0128); a work central to both appears in both.

Related resources. Checkable claim. Other open resources map philosophy or the philosophy of science by other means. The Indiana Philosophy Ontology (InPhO) builds a dynamic ontology of the discipline, starting from an encyclopedia and refined by expert feedback and crowdsourcing (Buckner, Niepert & Allen 2011; Eckert et al. 2010); Pasin and Motta (2011) set out an ontology for annotating philosophical resources; co-citation analysis has mapped representative epistemologists and philosophers of science by who is cited with whom (Kreuzman 2001); SOPHIS is an open database for quantitative studies of contemporary philosophy of science (Petrovich et al. 2026); the Comp HOPOS dataset gives metadata for the computational history of philosophy of science (Hicks, Morris & Brister 2019); and the PhilPapers Survey records the positions of professional philosophers on a set of questions (Bourget & Chalmers 2014). Proposal. This catalogue differs from them in coding works by hand on stated questions, with the reason for each code recorded, and in surveying works rather than people or citations, from antiquity to the 2020s and across several traditions. The comparison rests on the literature searched, not on a systematic review.

Selection. The selection is purposive, not systematic. It aims to represent each answer coded in §3 with at least one work that states it, and to include traditions outside Western philosophy where they bear directly on the questions. It under-represents work not available in English, and several traditions are entered through modern studies; Buddhist epistemology is entered only through one. §16 lists the main gaps.

Register. Each work has one entry in a shared register, with first publication, edition used, translator, summary, reading, codes and links. Bibliographic data were checked against publisher, journal, Crossref and library records; the state of the check for each work is recorded in the register and summarised in the provenance record published with the register. For classical texts, the register names a modern translation and the standard reference system (Stephanus, Bekker, AT, A/B and so on).

Coding. The three questions were coded by one coder, with AI assistance in applying the codebook (the provenance record describes it). The coder applied the codebook to each work’s answer as stated in its summary, decided every code, and checked that works giving the same answer carry the same code across the register. Each code and the reason for it are recorded in the coding log, published with the register. An intra-rater check, in which a random sample is re-coded after an interval and agreement is reported, is specified in the intra-rater protocol published with the register, and its sample has been drawn; the re-coding has not been carried out, so no reliability statistics are reported. No second coder was used. The three questions have no both code, so the table of counts under alternative codings in the Catalogue of Scientific Methods has no counterpart here; Appendix A works through one borderline case instead.

Statuses. The opening paragraph of each section says what the section contains. Each statement in the section notes (What this section adds), in §13, in the claims ledger (§14), in the open questions (§15) and in Appendix A carries a status: checkable claim (a statement about what a work says or what a study found, which a reader can check against the source), proposal (an organising device or reading the author offers), direction (a line worth pursuing, not yet argued in full), question, or illustration (a constructed example that reports no finding). A qualifier after a status names what the statement is about, as in checkable claim (of the register): a statement about how the register is coded or what it counts, which a reader checks by regenerating it from the register rather than against the works, and which is not a finding about the field. A passage with parts of different kinds gives each part its own status, as in §13.1.

Evidence grades. Where a checkable claim concerns empirical evidence, it also carries a grade from the evidence protocol used across the thoughts papers (codebook, §6.2). Strong: at least three independent studies, including at least one within-person or experimentally manipulated comparison; effects in the predicted direction; no major published contradiction. Moderate: at least two independent studies, at least one of which partly addresses a named confound; the remaining confound is stated. Suggestive: one direct study, or converging indirect evidence; the missing control is stated. Contested: directly conflicting results, or an unresolved methodological dispute, which is named. Open: no direct test located, or the only direct test located is a null result; the prediction and a design that would test it are stated. A grade attaches to the link between a claim and its evidence; where the text reports what a study found, it reports that study’s result. A qualifier in parentheses names the part of a claim that is graded, as in Contested (generalisation). Claims about interpretation or history carry no grade, marked “—”. No claim in this catalogue carries a grade in this version: its claims concern interpretation, history or the register’s own counts. The graded claims in the shared ledger are in the Catalogue of Scientific Methods (C-04, C-05).

What this catalogue is not. It is not a history of epistemology, and it does not judge which theory is right. It reports no new data.

Framework · Section 3

Codes for the three questions

The codebook, published with the register, defines each code with an example. In brief:

Question Codes
Location: what carries it mind · experience · language · body
Location: what arrangement produces it practice · community · institution · network · system
Location: from what position relation · situated · tradition · plural
Knower individual · expert · community · collective · situated · marginalised · nonhuman-inclusive · plural
Decision proof · test · probability · coherence · consensus · critique · dialogue · practice · pragmatic · power

Each work receives one code on each question; no work in this version is coded “not applicable” on any of them. Where a work fits more than one location code, the codebook’s priority rule decides: the position group if the work’s main claim is about the knower’s position, the arrangement group if it is about how knowledge is produced, and the carrier group otherwise (Appendix A applies it). Every code records what a work says or proposes, not the catalogue’s view. A work coded consensus may describe agreement as what in fact settles claims (R-0013) or propose it as the rational procedure (R-0224); a work coded power describes claims as settled by power; a work coded marginalised gives knowers in marginalised positions specific standing. Four names appear under more than one question: community, practice, situated and plural. Each is read as an answer to the question it is listed under. As a location, situated places knowledge in the knower’s social position; as a knower code, it says that all knowers are positioned, with no position privileged. A work can carry both (Appendix A); that is not double counting, since the two codes answer different questions.

Entries · Section 4

Ancient and classical traditions

The section brings together early accounts of knowledge from Greek, Indian, Islamic and Chinese philosophy, from the fifth century BCE to the fourteenth century CE. Several traditions are entered through modern studies as well as primary texts, because the primary texts are read through commentaries that a short entry cannot fully represent. Such entries carry a Summary basis line, and their codes describe the tradition as the study presents it. Where possible, two studies are entered, so that the coding of a tradition does not rest on one interpretation (Matilal and Phillips for Nyāya; Hansen and Graham for classical Chinese thought); Buddhist epistemology is entered through one study (R-0115) and Avicenna through one study (R-0216) beside his own text. The Nyāya-sūtra (R-0233), the Mozi (R-0234) and two chapters of Avicenna’s Book of Demonstration (R-0235) are entered as primary texts beside the studies, and Plato, Aristotle, al-Ghazālī and Ibn Khaldūn through their own texts in translation.

R-0234 · Mozi and the Mohist school (5th–3rd c. BCE). Mozi

Chinese · 5th c. BCE · treatise · claim type: conceptual

Summary. The writings of the Mohist school. The core chapters judge a doctrine by three standards (biao): its basis in the deeds of the sage-kings, its source in what people have seen and heard, and its use in benefiting the state and people (ch. 35). The later Canons name three sources of knowledge, hearsay (wen), explanation (shuo) and personal observation (qin) (A80), and define disputation (bian) as contending over which of two contrary terms fits a thing, the side whose term fits winning (A74).

Reading. The primary text behind the two Chinese studies in this section (Graham 1989, R-0217; Hansen 1992, R-0118). In the Canons a disputation ends when one term fits the thing; the question it opens is how that criterion stands to the plural daos of the Zhuangzi that both studies discuss.

Knowing: location language · knower community · decision test

Note. Canon numbers follow Graham’s arrangement (A, B), as used in the SEP entry ‘Mohist Canons’.

First published: Compiled in China over the Warring States period; the Canons (chs. 40–45) date from the late fourth to the mid third century BCE. Edition used: Johnston, I. (Trans.) (2010). The Mozi: A Complete Translation. Columbia University Press (Translations from the Asian Classics). Cited by chapter.

R-0112 · Plato (c. 369 BCE). Theaetetus

classical · 4th c. BCE · treatise · claim type: conceptual

Summary. Socrates and Theaetetus test three definitions of knowledge: perception, true judgement, and true judgement with an account (logos, 201c–d). The third is tested against three senses of ‘account’: putting one’s judgement into words, listing a thing’s elements, and stating the mark that distinguishes it from everything else (206c–210a). Each definition is refuted, and the dialogue ends without a definition (210a–b). The Meno (97e–98a) gives a related image: true opinions become knowledge when tied down by reasoning about the cause.

Reading. The dialogue poses the question of what must be added to true belief; it does not establish the justified true belief analysis, which is a much later construction (see C-02).

R-0113 · Aristotle (c. 350 BCE). Posterior Analytics [shared]

classical · 4th c. BCE · treatise · claim type: formal

Summary. Defines scientific knowledge (epistēmē) as knowledge of why a thing is so, through demonstration: a deduction from premises that are true, primary, immediate, better known than and prior to the conclusion, and causes of it (I.2, 71b20–22). First principles are not demonstrated but grasped by understanding (nous), reached from perception through induction (II.19).

Reading. Knowledge here belongs to one who grasps why a thing is so, and the chain of demonstration ends in principles that are understood, not proved. The account is structured as a model of a whole body of knowledge, which early modern works on method criticise (Bacon 1620, R-0002, and Galilei 1638, R-0189, both in the Catalogue of Scientific Methods).

Summary. The root text of the Nyāya school, read with the commentaries of Vātsyāyana, Uddyotakara and Vācaspati Miśra. R-0116 · al-Ghazālī, Abū Ḥāmid (c. 1106–1110). al-Munqidh min al-ḍalāl (Deliverance from Error)

Islamic · 12th c. · treatise · claim type: conceptual

Summary. An intellectual autobiography in which al-Ghazālī doubts sense perception and reason in search of certain knowledge, recovers trust in the necessary truths of reason not by argument but by a light that God cast into his breast, examines theology, philosophy and Ismāʿīlī teaching, and finds the highest certainty in the experiential knowledge (dhawq, ‘taste’) of the Sufis.

Reading. Descartes’s Meditations (R-0119) doubts in a comparable order and resolves the doubt by proof.

R-0117 · Ibn Khaldūn (1377). al-Muqaddimah (The Introduction)

Islamic · 14th c. · treatise · claim type: methodological

Summary. Sets out a critical method for history: reports must be tested against what is possible given the nature of human social organisation (ʿumrān), not accepted on the authority of their transmitters alone. Applies it, for example, to al-Masʿūdī’s report of more than 600,000 Israelite fighting men under Moses, which he rejects because Egypt and Syria could not have supported or deployed an army of that size. Reading. A test of testimony by social plausibility, which puts this work in conversation with the work on testimony in §8 (Coady 1992, R-0151; Lackey 2008, R-0225).

Decolonial, African and Indigenous epistemologies

These works examine how colonialism shaped what counts as knowledge and as a knower, or set out knowledge traditions that colonial institutions excluded. They run from Fanon through Latin American decolonial thought and Anzaldúa’s borderlands to an Aymara critique of decolonial discourse. They include a debate within African philosophy over whether a tradition can be treated as one shared worldview, with Oruka’s sage philosophy as a methodological answer, an African study of gender as a colonial category, and Māori, Native American and Hawaiian accounts of knowledge.

The typical answers do not use every code: no mode includes institution, tradition or plural as a location, plural as a knower, or probability, consensus or pragmatic as a decision. Read strictly, with all three of a work’s codes inside one mode’s typical answers, no work fits two modes and many fit none; those works are read from the answers they share with one or more modes. Among them are three works that locate knowledge in a community and decide by critique, as the reflexive-standpoint mode does, but take a knower outside its answers: Longino (R-0057) a community, Hegel (R-0123) a collective and Hountondji (R-0160) individuals; the two other works with that location and decision, Fricker and Dotson, fit the mode.

Proposal. A disagreement read from one mode can look like a mistake by the other side. Read from two or more, it can turn out to be a difference in the answer to one of the three questions (C-13).

13.5 A worked case: two readings of the same evidence

Illustration. Suppose two research groups look at the same data: children who speak languages with transparent number words (such as ‘ten-one’ for eleven) make fewer errors on some early arithmetic tasks than children whose languages use opaque forms. Group 1 reads this as an effect of language structure on cognitive cost. Group 2 reads it as an effect of schooling, practice and expectation, which differ between the countries compared. The case is constructed for illustration; it does not report a finding. Read from one mode alone, each reading can look like the other side’s mistake: from the analytic-foundational mode, Group 2’s reading can look like a hypothesis not yet stated so that it makes different predictions; from the reflexive-standpoint mode, Group 1’s reading can look like one that takes some languages as the baseline for all.

Mode What it asks of the disagreement
Analytic-foundational Are the two hypotheses stated so that they make different predictions? Is either reading circular?
Empirical-pragmatic Which comparison would separate them: bilingual children tested in each language; matched schooling; an intervention on number naming (Mill 1843, R-0125; Fisher 1935, R-0025 in the Catalogue of Scientific Methods)?
Reflexive-standpoint Whose languages are taken as the baseline? Who designed the tasks, and in which language were they first written? Do the communities whose children are tested recognise the description (Smith 1999, R-0069)?
Phenomenological-enactive What is it like for a child to count in each system: where does the effort show itself, in speech, in fingers, in writing (Noë 2004, R-0178)?
Relational-systemic Which parts of the system carry the arithmetic: the words, the written numerals, the abacus, the teacher, the curriculum (Hutchins 1995, R-0080)?

Proposal. Read through the three questions, the two groups can be read as disagreeing mainly about the first: Group 1 places the relevant knowledge partly in language, Group 2 in practice and institutions. Group 2’s reading is also a claim about the evidence: that the countries compared differ in more than their languages. Making that explicit does not settle the dispute, but it shows which evidence would bear on it.

13.6 Constraint as a lens

Direction. A position in the register can also be described by what it rules out. Illustration: foundationalism rules out chains of justification that never reach a basic belief; empiricism, claims with no basis in experience; standpoint theory, claims that ignore the knower’s position; enactivism, accounts of cognition detached from a body. Checkable claim. Popper (R-0009) rules out, as unscientific, a theory that forbids no possible observation; Smith (R-0069) rules out research whose questions and terms of evidence are set without the community it concerns. Neither exclusion appears in a definition of knowledge. Describing a theory of knowledge by its constraints, rather than by its definition of knowledge, may make theories easier to compare across traditions. This catalogue records the idea as a direction; it is not developed here (C-15).

C-02

Status: checkable claim · Grade: —

The Theaetetus examines and rejects the definition of knowledge as true judgement with an account (201c–210b) and ends without a definition; it does not inaugurate a ‘justified true belief’ analysis. That analysis was set out in mid-twentieth-century analytic epistemology, chiefly by those who criticised it; Dutant (2015) argues that the traditional conception of knowledge was a different one, which required a discernible mark of truth. Gettier (1963) gave two counterexamples to the analysis, and cases of this kind have since been called Gettier cases. Cases of true belief that falls short of knowledge appear earlier: in Indian philosophy (the Buddhist Dharmottara, c. 770 CE; the Advaita Vedāntin Śrīharṣa, 12th c.), in Peter of Mantua (14th c.), and in Russell (1912, ch. XIII; 1948, Part II, ch. V).

Basis: Plato c. 369 BCE, R-0112; Gettier 1963, R-0134; Dutant 2015, R-0219; Russell 1912, R-0218

Further sources: Ichikawa, J. J., & Steup, M. (2001; substantive revision 2026); Dreyfus, G. B. J. (1997); Das, N. (2018); Boh, I. (1985); Russell, B. (1948)

Check at: Theaetetus 201c–210b; Dutant 2015, pp. 95–145; Ichikawa & Steup, §1 (on the traditional analysis) and §3; Dreyfus 1997, p. 292 (Dharmottara); Das 2018, §2.1 (Śrīharṣa); Boh 1985, p. 95 (Peter of Mantua); Russell 1912, ch. XIII; Russell 1948, Part II, ch. V, p. 113

What would change it: Evidence that the JTB analysis was widely held as such before the mid-twentieth century.

Section 16

Limitations and closing

Limitations.

  • Selection. The selection is purposive, by one person, and weighted toward work in English or English translation. Indian, Islamic, Chinese and African epistemologies are each represented by three or four works, which is not proportionate to their literatures. Latin American philosophy is represented mainly by decolonial thought. The table below lists the main gaps, each with a representative work, ranked by the author’s estimate of how much their inclusion would change the counts in §13.1; the estimate is not computed.
  • Contributors. The register was coded and its summaries drafted by one person, and it leans toward the Western academic tradition. Its depth, its coverage and the reliability of its codes would all gain from more contributors, above all specialists in the traditions it under-represents. Proposals of works, summaries and codes are invited.
  • Modern studies standing for traditions. Several traditions are entered through modern studies as well as primary texts: Matilal 1986, R-0114, Phillips 2012, R-0215 and Dunne 2004, R-0115 (Indian); Hansen 1992, R-0118 and Graham 1989, R-0217 (Chinese); McGinnis 2010, R-0216 (Islamic). Buddhist epistemology is entered only through one study, Dunne’s. Such entries carry a Summary basis line, and their codes describe the tradition as the study presents it.
  • Summaries and readings. Summaries compress whole works; readings are the author’s and are marked.
  • Coding. Codes are the decisions of one coder; no second coder was used, and the intra-rater check, whose sample is drawn, has not been carried out.
  • No new data. The catalogue reports no new data.

Glossary

agential realism
The view that phenomena are produced by intra-actions of apparatus and object, so that what becomes determinate depends on the arrangement of the inquiry. See Barad 2007, R-0067.

apt belief
A belief that is accurate because of the believer’s competence. See Sosa 2007, R-0138.

autopoiesis
The organisation of a living system as a network of processes that produce the components that produce it. See Maturana & Varela 1980, R-0085.

coherence
Mutual support among claims within a system, used as a standard of acceptance alongside or instead of empirical test. As the name of pole B of axis 2 in the Catalogue of Scientific Methods, it covers five non-empirical standards, including demonstration and social closure (§3 of that catalogue). See Quine 1951, R-0008; Dawid 2013, R-0096 in the Catalogue of Scientific Methods.

coloniality of power
A pattern of global power, organised around racial classification, that outlasted formal colonialism and includes control of knowledge. See Quijano 2000, R-0163.

constructive empiricism
The view that science aims at empirical adequacy, truth about what is observable, not truth about the unobservable. See van Fraassen 1980, R-0042.

contextualism
The view that the standards a belief must meet to count as knowledge vary with the context in which knowledge is attributed. See Lewis 1996, R-0222.

discursive formation
The historically specific rules that determine which statements can be made, by whom and about what. See Foucault 1969, R-0149.

distributed cognition
Cognition carried out by a system of people, artefacts and procedures rather than by one mind. See Hutchins 1995, R-0080.

double consciousness
Du Bois’s term for the experience of seeing oneself through the eyes of a society that looks on in contempt. See Du Bois 1903, R-0226.

ecology of knowledges
The coexistence of plural knowledges related by intercultural translation rather than ranked. See Santos 2014, R-0165.

enactivism
The view that cognition is embodied action that brings forth a world, not representation of a pregiven one. See Varela et al. 1991, R-0175; Thompson 2007, R-0176.

epistemic injustice
A wrong done to someone in their capacity as a knower: testimonial (credibility deflated by prejudice) or hermeneutical (a gap in shared concepts caused by structural identity prejudice). See Fricker 2007, R-0158.

epistemicide
The destruction or suppression of knowledge systems. See Santos 2014, R-0165.

ethnophilosophy
The presentation of a collective, implicit worldview as a people’s philosophy; criticised by Hountondji. See Hountondji 1977, R-0160.

extended mind
The thesis that cognitive processes can include resources outside the body that play the same role as internal ones. See Clark & Chalmers 1998, R-0232.

fallacy of misplaced concreteness
Mistaking an abstraction for concrete reality. See Whitehead 1925, R-0183.

fallibilism
The view that any knowledge claim may be revised. See Peirce 1877, R-0126.

foundherentism
A theory of justification combining experiential evidence (foundationalism) with mutual support among beliefs (coherentism). See Haack 1993, R-0221.

fusion of horizons
Understanding as the meeting of the interpreter’s horizon with that of a text or tradition. See Gadamer 1960, R-0133.

Gettier case
A case of justified true belief that is true by luck and so not knowledge. See Gettier 1963, R-0134.

interactional expertise
Fluency in a field’s language without the ability to practise it. See Collins & Evans 2007, R-0052.

internal realism
The view that truth is idealised rational acceptability within a conceptual scheme. See Putnam 1981, R-0140.

justified true belief
The analysis of knowledge as belief that is true and justified; the target of Gettier’s counterexamples. Plato’s Theaetetus examines a related definition, true judgement with an account, and rejects it (C-02). See Gettier 1963, R-0134; Dutant 2015, R-0219; Plato c. 369 BCE, R-0112.

knowledge first
The view that knowledge is not analysable into belief plus conditions and is prior to belief and justification. See Williamson 2000, R-0223.

language game
A practice of using language, embedded in a form of life, in which words have their meaning. See Wittgenstein 1953, R-0132.

mechanical objectivity
The ideal of removing the observer’s intervention from the production of scientific images and data. See Daston & Galison 1992, R-0167 in the Catalogue of Scientific Methods; Daston & Galison 2007, R-0168.

mestiza consciousness
Anzaldúa’s term for a way of knowing formed by living between cultures, tolerant of contradiction. See Anzaldúa 1987, R-0231.

mode of existence
In Latour, a form of truth-making (such as reference, law or fiction) with its own conditions of truth and falsity. See Latour 2012, R-0186.

naturalised epistemology
Epistemology pursued as part of empirical science, especially psychology. See Quine 1969, R-0135.

peer disagreement
Disagreement with someone judged equally informed and competent. See Christensen & Lackey 2013, R-0153.

power/knowledge
Foucault’s term for the joint production of knowledge about individuals and power over them. See Foucault 1975, R-0150.

pramāṇa
In Indian philosophy, a means of valid knowledge, such as perception or inference. See Matilal 1986, R-0114; Dunne 2004, R-0115.

praxiography
The study of how objects are enacted in practices. See Mol 2002, R-0171.

reliabilism
The view that a belief is justified if produced by a process that tends to produce true beliefs. See Goldman 1979, R-0136.

sage philosophy
Oruka’s method of documenting the critical reflection of individual sages within oral traditions. See Oruka 1990, R-0230.

sensitivity (tracking)
The condition that if p were false, the believer would not believe p; Nozick’s addition to true belief. See Nozick 1981, R-0220.

situated knowledges
Knowledge that is partial, embodied and accountable for its location, offered as a form of objectivity. See Haraway 1988, R-0062.

standpoint
A position, achieved through critical work from a social location, from which relations hidden by dominant perspectives become visible. See Hartsock 1983, R-0154; Harding 1986, R-0063.

strong objectivity
Objectivity that includes critical scrutiny of the researcher’s own background assumptions, starting from marginalised lives. See Harding 1991, R-0064.

strong programme
The programme in the sociology of knowledge that explains true and false beliefs by the same types of cause (symmetry). See Bloor 1976, R-0145; Barnes 1974, R-0144.

testimony
Knowledge acquired from what others tell us. See Coady 1992, R-0151.

underdetermination
The situation in which evidence does not by itself settle which of several theories to accept. See Duhem 1906, R-0007 in the Catalogue of Scientific Methods; Quine 1951, R-0008.

virtue epistemology
Epistemology that analyses knowledge through the competences or character of the knower. See Zagzebski 1996, R-0137; Sosa 2007, R-0138.

warranted assertibility
Dewey’s term for the outcome of successful inquiry, in place of truth fixed in advance. See Dewey 1938, R-0128.
